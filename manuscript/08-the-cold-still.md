## Chapter 8: The Cold Still

Margaret Brennan raked red oak coals flat across her stone hearth. Over the Dutch-oven lid went a clean cap of gray wood ash so the corn pone inside would crust brown without scorching.

White March fog pressed thick against the four-pane kitchen glass. She lifted her stoneware crock of rendered mutton tallow from the shelf. Its wire bail hooked onto her small brass steelyard. Four pounds even balanced the notch: thirty winter evenings of dipped rush-candles if nobody burned a wick past nine o’clock.

Margaret set the crock on her scoured poplar table. Wiping her fingers on her apron, she checked the room by what stood empty.

An hour before daybreak twenty-one-year-old Caleb had eaten by the chimney. He had tucked two thick wedges of cold cornbread inside his coat and ridden up Beech Creek after two strayed heifers. His clasp-knife and greased shot-pouch were gone from the mantelboard.

Five minutes back her husband Jacob had carried the cedar water-pail and her ash butter-paddle down to the springhouse. Beside the trammel hook his tin cup still steamed half full of black parched-corn coffee.

Behind the battened oak door, nineteen-year-old Matthew’s hickory peg hung bare.

Underneath sat Matthew’s everyday brogans with Friday’s red clay still crusted stiff along the heels. Yesterday noon the boy had oiled his stitched Sunday shoes, buttoned his heavy winter coat, and walked up the road to help Joshua dress the millstones. Neither boy nor bay mule had come back through her gate at supper. Up at the loft ladder-head, the small iron candlestick stood cold. Its tallow stub remained unburned.

Margaret untied the narrow cotton tape inside her apron band. Out came a small brass key. She unlocked her dovetailed red-cedar box on the mantel shelf and lifted the lid.

On top lay her home-stitched household book, sewn from clean half-sheets. Nobody else in Elk Cove read her hand. She kept her house in short upright strokes, initials, and cipher marks of her own. Dipping a pared goose-quill into the corked walnut-gall bottle, she entered yesterday’s two dozen bartered eggs from North Fork and this morning’s four pounds of clear tallow.

While the brown ink dried, her palm rested on two folded sheets beneath the book.

Bottommost lay a heavy rag-paper deed dated November of eighteen fifty-four and headed *State of North Carolina, County of Watauga*, conveying forty acres of creek bottom and chestnut slope on Elk Cove to her father in fee simple. Six springs back, on the twenty-third of March in seventy-three, fire had taken the wooden Watauga courthouse over at Boone down to red brick and white ash. Every antebellum deed book in that county went up the chimney in smoke. No other copy of her father’s root title existed anywhere on earth.

Above it lay her own crisp postwar deed recorded down at Bakersville in sixty-eight, setting apart those same forty acres as her separate estate under Article Ten, Section Six of the new state constitution.

Cold mist drifted through the half-open doorway across wet grass. Forty paces down the gentle slope, the breadth of a fenced cabbage patch, stood her father’s hewn white-oak log barn. Both heavy plank doors hung shut on wooden pins. Not a thread of woodsmoke rose through the wet shingles. Not a sour breath of cooked corn hung on the air. Midnight Saturday the fire under that brick arch had gone out cold.

Twenty winters Margaret had boiled Jacob’s mash-stiffened shirts every Monday morning. Every autumn she had tallied to the peck what coarse chop came down Joshua’s cart road past the forked white oak. Equally plain stood the fifty-pound bags of Kanawha salt, green coffee, and heavy Piedmont shirting brought back from Cranberry Forks without a silver coin leaving the shelf.

No clerk had needed to tell her why an eighty-gallon copper kettle sat bricked inside her father’s log barn instead of Joshua’s mortgaged mill-lot. Under North Carolina law no storekeeper’s judgment touched a wife’s separate land. Nor could any husband lease or charge one foot of his wife’s dirt unless a county clerk or magistrate took her privy examination apart from her husband and certified her free consent under seal.

Twenty years she had kept that wall whole with her own two shoes. Never once since the copper went in had Margaret set her foot across the white-oak sill of that lower barn. Never once had her hand touched a jug. Never had she signed a paper at Bakersville. Store creditors stopped at her fence. County sheriffs stopped at her fence.

Margaret shut the stitched book over both deeds. She locked the cedar box. Back inside her apron band went the brass key.

Down at the shallow creek ford, iron horseshoes splashed loud through unseen water. Curb-chains chinked sharp on wet steel. High in the blind cloud on Beech Knob, no bone call sounded.

---

Six horses and a pack-animal rode out of the fog into Margaret’s yard.

In front on a tall bay gelding rode a neat-bearded man in a dark cape. Close at his stirrup came a broad-shouldered Deputy United States Marshal in a belted frieze overcoat with a black oilcloth parcel strapped tight behind his saddle. Behind them rode a gray-bearded gentleman carrying a brass-cornered case. Last came two older Bakersville freeholders holding a ruled pine tally-board and one younger hired deputy cradling a blued lever carbine across his thigh.

Ten yards short of the porch steps, the rider in the cape halted. Once he glanced back down the empty fog-choked creek road toward the turnpike, where a seventh horseman had not come up. Turning toward the house, he swung lightly down from his gelding.

Up the slate path from the springhouse walked forty-five-year-old Jacob Brennan.

In his left hand Jacob carried the brimming cedar pail of spring water. In his right he held Margaret’s clean ash butter-paddle. Six strange horses blocked his yard between chopping block and gate. Jacob did not check his stride. Not a drop spilled over his boot. Walking straight to the lower porch bench, he set the full pail level on the oak plank, laid the butter-paddle dry across the rim, and wiped both damp palms down his jeans trousers.

Forty paces below the bank his barn stood cold and empty. Under his boots lay forty acres carrying no Brennan man’s name on the tax roll. Jacob turned square toward the bay gelding. Both hands hung open at his sides.

Margaret stepped onto the edge of her kitchen porch. Both flour-dry hands folded flat over the brass key at her waist.

William Parrish lifted his wet slouch hat an inch toward the porch. “Good morning, Madam,” he said quietly. Turning half round to the yard, he looked at her husband. “Jacob Brennan?”

“I am,” Jacob said.

“William Parrish, United States Internal Revenue Agent,” Parrish said without raising his voice. “This officer is Deputy United States Marshal Hawkins out of Asheville, holding a commissioner’s search and arrest warrant sworn at Bakersville. Stand here beside your block while the gauger examines your lower barn.”

Jacob gave one short nod. Neither he nor Margaret looked down the grass path.

Gauger Kerr dismounted with his brass-cornered case. Followed by the Deputy Marshal and the armed posseman, Kerr walked the forty paces down through the wet weeds and pulled the wooden peg from the white-oak barn door.

Heavy split planks swung wide into the mist. Inside the dark bay brass clinked hollow against cold sheet copper. Wood scraped dry sour crust down the staves of empty tubs.

Margaret did not stir from her upper step. Down by the chopping block Jacob stood quiet with his thumbs hooked at his belt, watching fog drift off the wet orchard limbs.

Five minutes passed. Out of the barn came the Deputy Marshal and Gauger Kerr. Dry hay-chaff clung to their dark sleeves. Inside the barn behind them, the hired deputy lifted a heavy poll-axe. Iron bit loud through seasoned cypress wood. Blow after blow echoed flat up the slope. Six fermenting tubs split stave from hoop across the earthen floor.

The Deputy Marshal walked up to the chopping block and unfolded a printed sheet. From his belt he unclipped a pair of hinged iron wrist-shackles.

“United States against Jacob Brennan,” the Deputy Marshal read out briskly. “Warrant upon oath under Sections thirty-two fifty-eight, thirty-two sixty-six, and thirty-two eighty-one of the Revised Statutes: having in custody one set-up eighty-gallon copper still unregistered with the Collector; using a distilling boiler inside a barn connected with a dwelling-house; and carrying on the business of a distiller without bond. Put out your wrists.”

Jacob held both callused wrists forward together without a word. Steel ratchets clicked twice. Once only Jacob lifted his eyes toward Margaret on the porch in a flat look that asked no question and named her in nothing. Then he turned toward his brown pack-mare under the shed so the Marshal could saddle her for the twenty-two-mile ride to Bakersville jail.

Parrish turned back toward the porch steps, hat in hand. Behind him waited the two Bakersville appraisers with board and pencil.

“Under Section thirty-four fifty-three, Madam,” Parrish said evenly, “the sworn appraisers are required to walk through connected structures to verify no unstamped spirits stand concealed indoors and to note personal chattels upon the schedule. Give them room by the hearth, and nothing of yours will be disordered.”

Margaret stepped silently two paces left beside her stone jamb. Both arms stayed folded across her apron band.

Both Bakersville men scraped their boots once and crossed her poplar floor. Twelve dark crescents of red clay marked the pale boards. Margaret priced every track on the wood: three kettles of boiling spring water, two scoops of strong hickory-ash lye, and sharp creek sand on her knees to scour that stain clean.

The elder appraiser, old Abner Vance from down near Cane Creek, lifted the meal-bench cloth and peered behind the four-post ash loom. Not a jug, funnel, or copper measure sat inside the four walls. Vance straightened his back and nodded to his companion.

“Set down the room schedule,” Vance muttered.

Scratch went the lead pencil down the ruled foolscap sheet.

“One four-post ash loom with reed and shuttles — four dollars,” the younger man read aloud as he wrote. “One cast-iron bake-oven with lid and pot-hooks — one dollar twenty. Six split-bottom hickory chairs — one dollar fifty.”

Vance stepped to the foot of the corded bedstead. He hooked two fingers under the walnut lid of the cedar chest. Up swung the heavy board.

Dry mountain lavender rose faint into the damp air. Folded square on top of the white winter quilts lay one dark-blue homespun wool shawl, spun fine before the war, its frayed lower fringe darned neat with black thread and fastened at the fold with a plain oval brass pin. Seventeen springs that shawl had lain untouched at the top of Margaret’s chest since a five-year-old boy came across the branch to eat at her table.

Vance’s companion squinted down at the mended wool. He tapped his pencil against his teeth. Then he wrote the next line and spoke the price flat.

“One folded dark wool shawl, brass-pinned — forty cents.”

Forty cents stood beside the pin: two dozen autumn eggs laid across Hoss Tyler’s counter, or half a short winter day at the spinning wheel.

Margaret unfolded her arms. Two smooth steps carried her between the man’s elbow and the chest. Her palm came down on the walnut lid. Flat it shut without a slam. Looking past both appraisers, she spoke straight to William Parrish at her threshold.

“Everything inside these four walls came out of my father’s house before I married, Mister Parrish,” Margaret said in a clear, level voice. “There is no whiskey jug in my chest.”

Silence held the kitchen for three seconds. Down the slope, the poll-axe struck one last blow against the brick arch. Everything went quiet.

Parrish looked from Margaret’s steady eyes to the shut cedar lid. Then he glanced at the ruled board under the appraiser’s thumb.

“Draw your lead through every household item inside this dwelling, Mister Vance,” Parrish said quietly. “Our seizure runs to the distilling apparatus, the mash tubs, and the real estate. Leave the woman’s house linen off your sheet.”

Black pencil lead scored two straight lines across the loom, the Dutch oven, the six hickory chairs, and the forty-cent shawl. Both appraisers turned without meeting Margaret’s eyes. Out onto the porch they stepped.

---

High in the dripping laurel above the pasture rails, loose slate rattled loud.

Wet branches crashed apart. Over the top rail vaulted Joshua Brennan on foot, mud plastered thick to both knees and one coat shoulder ripped open by briars, hauling his lathered claybank mare down the steep grass bank by her bit-ring.

Down by the barn the hired deputy swung his carbine halfway up. Parrish raised one gloved hand shoulder-high without turning his head. Down dropped the barrel toward the mud.

Joshua’s boots slid to a halt ten paces inside the gate. White steam rose thick off his mare’s heaving flanks.

Across the wet yard Joshua’s eyes struck the iron shackles on Jacob’s wrists. Five seconds passed with no sound save the mare’s rough wind and cold drops falling from the eaves. Joshua looked once from his brother’s chained hands up to Margaret on her step.

Beside the chopping block Jacob lifted both shackled wrists an inch.

“Where’s Matthew?” Jacob asked once.

Joshua’s eyes dropped straight to the red clay between his boots. Not a muscle in his bearded jaw moved. He gave his brother no answer.

Before Jacob spoke again, heavy oak sled-runners grated slow in the bend. Up through the white fog clinked an iron ox-yoke ring.

Through the open yard gate walked sixty-year-old William Harper, bareheaded in the cold mist, leading his yoke of big red steers by the horn-rope. Close beside the low oak tailboard walked Harper’s eldest son Luke, steadying a folded gray horse-blanket against the pitch of the ruts. Ten paces behind the sled rode the seventh man from Parrish’s party, the third hired deputy on the Marshal’s roll. White as lard around the mouth, the man sat stiff in his saddle with his blued carbine across his pommel and the broken neck-ring of a two-gallon gray stoneware jug dangling on rawhide from his horn.

Harper halted his steers square in the middle of Margaret’s yard. He did not glance at Parrish or the Deputy Marshal. Walking back to the sled, Harper took the edge of clean meal-sacking. Both weathered hands folded the cloth back to the waist.

Lying flat on his side in dry oat-straw was fifteen-year-old Lish Councill.

Old Amos Councill’s grandson wore his dead father’s cut-down homespun jeans coat buttoned neat with horn disks to his thin throat. Between his narrow shoulder blades the gray wool stood soaked black and stiff. Around his neck on waxed hemp twine hung the hollowed gobbler wing-bone, unblown. Two bare chapped red wrists lay crossed still above split yarn mittens. Beside his knee stuck three wet shards of gray jug-stone coated with red lane mud.

Down by the chopping block Jacob lunged half a step against his chains. Before the Deputy Marshal’s hand reached his pistol holster, Joshua stepped hard across the grass and planted one broad palm flat against Jacob’s breastbone, holding his brother still with all his weight so no gun lifted in the yard.

Up on the porch Margaret made no sound. Neither hand went to her face.

She turned back into her kitchen and crossed to the walnut press. Pulling open the deep bottom drawer, she lifted out her best sun-bleached white linen bed-sheet, woven tight on her own four-post loom and kept folded in sweet clover for sickness or burying. With the white linen across her arm, she walked down the wet steps. Past the revenue horses she went and knelt in the mud beside Harper’s sled.

Carefully she lifted the rough hemp sacking clear of Lish Councill’s cold cheek. Once she shook out the white sheet from crown to boot-heel. Soft and smooth she tucked the hem beneath the boy’s chin and folded both sides firm under his thin shoulders so coarse tow-cloth would not rub widow Sarah Councill’s child on the rocky two-mile pull up North Fork Hollow.

William Parrish walked slowly across the yard to the sled tailboard. Removing his slouch hat completely, he held it down against his wet cape.

“Behind your lower barn, Mister Harper?” Parrish asked quietly.

William Harper kept both hands on the oak standard of his sled. Straight ahead he looked between his steers’ horns. “In my back cow-lane along the laurel fence,” Harper said, every word flat as cold anvil iron. “Boy came cutting across from the branch trail carrying a two-gallon jug he never made. Dropped the jug on the rocks when your hired man shouted, spun round to bolt into the brush, and took a rifle ball square between his shoulders.”

Back on his horse by the gate, the third deputy wet his lips. “Fog hung thick as sheep’s wool, Mister Parrish,” the man muttered huskily. “Looked like a grown man running for cover.”

Parrish did not look round at the horseman. From his dark coat breast came a narrow black-leather pocket memorandum book. Uncapping a brass lead pencil four feet from where Margaret rose upright with wet red clay soaking both knees of her apron, Parrish stood bareheaded at the sled tail and wrote in his neat clerk’s hand.

Plain in black lead stood the entry before he shut the cover.

“Fourth of March — one eighty-gallon copper still and six mash tubs seized at Brennan barn; one prisoner bound over; one boy, killed running at junction barn.”

Parrish slipped the shut book back over his heart.

---

By nine o’clock the yard stood empty down three wet roads.

South toward Bakersville jail rode the Deputy Marshal leading Jacob’s brown pack-mare. Jacob sat straight in his saddle with ironed wrists resting quiet on the pommel, bound over to answer before the federal court at Statesville on Monday, the twenty-first of April. Behind them plodded the pack-horse carrying the cold eighty-gallon kettle and coiled worm.

North up the steep creek trace toward North Fork creaked Harper’s red steers. Joshua walked silent in the mud at the off side of the sled, one broad hand braced steady against the white-sheeted shoulder so the dead boy would not slide on the ledges before reaching his mother’s gate.

High up Beech Creek, twenty-one-year-old Caleb had not yet come down off the mountain.

All through the gray noon hours Margaret worked alone without sitting down once. Three iron kettles of spring water came to a boil over oak coals. She poured two dippers of biting wood-ash lye into her cedar scrubbing-tub. Down on a folded sack with her stiff shuck brush she scoured all twelve revenue mud-tracks out of the pale poplar floorboards until the wet planks smelled clean as split timber. She washed Jacob’s cold tin cup and scalded it clean. Dry and bottom-up it went onto the shelf.

At four o’clock slow iron mule-shoes clicked alone up the cart road.

Outside her gate halted sixteen-year-old Thomas Harper, William Harper’s youngest boy, holding a hemp halter-rope.

Behind the boy stood Matthew’s young bay mule.

The worn leather saddle rode empty on its back. Both iron stirrups hung crossed neat over the wooden horn.

Margaret dried her red-chapped hands on a towel and walked to the gate.

“Mister Tyler asked me to lead him home when I came past the store, ma’am,” young Thomas said low, eyes respectful on the wet gate-latch. “Found him tied behind the corn-crib after the noon mail hack rolled west over State Line Gap toward Elizabethton and Johnson City.”

“Thank your father kindly, Thomas,” Margaret said evenly.

She took the damp rope from the boy and led the mule round to the shed. Unbuckling the girth, she lifted the empty saddle onto its peg and poured two full quarts of shelled yellow corn, one evening bait, into the wooden feed-box. An animal standing under her roof still ate before dark.

By six o’clock gray March dusk settled windless into Elk Cove. Halfway up the black hemlock wall of Beech Knob the river-fog hung torn like wet wool, leaving every bare oak twig beaded heavy with cold water. Inside her swept kitchen Jacob’s split-bottom chair stood pushed square under the table. Behind the door Matthew’s hickory peg hung empty. Beside the warm hearth Caleb’s covered corn pone waited on a clean pewter plate.

Alone between the silent house and the creek, Margaret stepped off her porch. No shawl covered her shoulders.

Slowly she walked the forty paces down the frost-stiffened grass path. One boot-length short of her father’s barn doorsill she halted. Twenty winters her feet had kept that exact line.

Inside the dark doorway lay shattered cypress staves, black brick, and sour puddles. Eye-high on the weathered white-oak doorpost, driven tight into her father’s timber with four bright cut-iron tacks, hung a broad white printed sheet.

Leaning close in the failing light, Margaret read every line under her breath.

“United States Internal Revenue, Sixth Collection District of North Carolina — Notice of Seizure. Take notice that there has been seized this fourth day of March, eighteen seventy-nine, as forfeited to the United States under the Act of the twentieth of July, eighteen sixty-eight, one eighty-gallon copper still, one worm, six mash tubs, and all that certain lot, tract, or parcel of land containing forty acres, more or less, situate on Elk Cove Creek in Cranberry Township, County of Mitchell, State of North Carolina, together with the log barn, outbuildings, ways, waters, and premises connected therewith; and all persons claiming any right, title, or interest in said real estate or personalty are commanded to appear and make due answer and cost-bond before the Circuit Court of the United States for the Western District of North Carolina at Statesville on the third Monday of April, eighteen seventy-nine, or suffer default, condemnation, and public sale.”

Ninety wagon-miles led down the mountains to Statesville, three full days in the saddle each way across three county lines. Answering took a clerk’s cost-bond in silver coin, with forty acres resting on one folded Watauga sheet upstairs that no burned courthouse could replace.

Margaret pulled none of the four bright tacks out of the oak. Standing still in the cold grass, she read the printed sheet a second time from top line to signature until every statute date, boundary word, and April Monday sat whole inside her head.

Then she turned from the open barn, walked the forty paces back up the dark path into her clean kitchen, and dropped the heavy oak bar across her door for the night.
