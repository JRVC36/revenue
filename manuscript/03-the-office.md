## Chapter 3: The Office

An hour before day Joshua broke the skim ice in the stable trough with his boot heel. Cold water slopped dark over the split poplar rim. He led the claybank mare out by her rope halter and let her drink four long swallows before taking the chill bit into her mouth.

Alone in the pole lot, he buckled the rawhide girth by feel. Frost stood stiff as coarse salt along the saddle skirts. Behind the cantle he tied a homespun poke holding four quarts of his own shelled white corn so he would not pay town stable price at noon. Across the dark race no smoke rose yet from Margaret’s chimney. Down by the branch the creek willows round her lower barn stood black and motionless in the rime.

He touched his vest pocket once. Inside rode the folded white half-sheet from Bakersville beside a thumb-stub of mill chalk. Twenty-two miles down the North Toe and twenty-two back before midnight took a whole clear grinding day out of October. Four turns of the pond went unground while shelled corn waited in neighbor cribs and the tenth of November walked closer three cents at a time.

Joshua checked the oak wedge on his shut headgate so the pond would fill while he rode. Then he led the mare through the slip-bars, dropped the top rail into its mortise, and mounted from the block.

Down at the tailrace bridge William Harper’s brown mare already stood blowing twin plumes of white steam across the frosted planks. Harper wore his heavy butternut riding coat buttoned to the throat. Canvas saddlebags rode behind his thighs. No rifle lay across his pommel.

“Pond froze at the edge?” Harper asked quietly.

“Skimmed thin by the gate,” Joshua said.

They turned south together along the cove trace without another word.

At the turnpike fork Hoss Tyler’s frame storehouse sat shuttered under the hemlocks, though a yellow tallow dip moved behind the oiled paper of the counting-room window. Iron scale-weights clinked once inside as Tyler weighed out blasting powder or coarse salt for an early teamster. Neither horseman drew rein. Below the store their horses’ shoes rang sharp on frozen red ruts, then snapped thin ice skinning the mule puddles where ore wagons had churned past Cranberry Forge.

River fog hung thick over the bloomery dam, though the big undershot wheel was already turning inside its ice-slicked frame. Every four breaths the trip-hammer fell with a dull iron thud that shook wet hemlock needles off the boughs along the road. Under the open shed roof two soot-blackened striker boys dragged a white-hot loop of charcoal iron from the hearth to the anvil block with long tongs. Across the yard, stacked neat under the porch eaves of the company office out of the frost, leaned six red-and-white level rods and three iron-shod surveyor’s tripods left overnight by the Pennsylvania party. Beyond Cranberry Creek the road climbed out of the laurel gorge and crossed the wide, treeless frost-flat called the Old Fields of Toe. Dead sedge stood knee-high in silver rime. Crows walked stiff-legged among the dry cattle droppings without rising as the two mares trotted past.

First sun struck the high chestnut ridges as they dropped into the crooked valley of the North Toe.

Teams were moving early on the county road. Out of a side hollow came two yoke of red steers hauling boxed sheet mica from the Clarissa pits toward the Marion railhead. Wire bound the pine crates tight. Loose mica flakes dusted the wagon bed and caught the low sun like fish scales in the frozen ruts. Half a mile lower an old man from Plumtree walked beside an ox-sled piled with dried apple quarters sewed up in tow sheets. Every driver lifted two fingers off whipstock or rein as Joshua and Harper passed. Nobody halted on an open bank to ask courthouse business.

North of Ledger hill the road benched above a rocky bend of the river. Twenty acres of cleared south slope lay grown up shoulder-high in sassafras sprouts and blackberry briars behind a rotted chestnut worm-fence. Halfway up the brush stood a roofless stone chimney. Nailed to the roadside white oak hung a weathered pine board split down the grain, its black stenciled letters washed gray by seven winters since the sheriff cried the place off at the courthouse door for unpaid township land tax under the sixty-eight law.

Harper looked once at the split board as his mare took the grade. He spat clean over the off stirrup.

“Three dollars and eighty cents tax and costs took old man Mace’s eighty acres,” Harper said. “Bought in by a town clerk from Morganton who never set a bull-tongue plow to one furrow of it.”

“Mace couldn’t read the print on the post door,” Joshua said.

“Read plain enough when the deputy brought the writ,” Harper said.

On the windy crest of Ledger hill they breathed the mares five minutes in the oak shade. Standing by their stirrups, they ate dry corn pone and cold fried middling out of greased paper, wiped their knives on dead grass, and swung back into leather. Below them the south pitch dropped two miles into the Cane Creek valley, where clear water ran a foot deep and sixteen feet wide over bright gravel straight into Bakersville.

---

By half past eleven six saddled horses and three mud-caked road mules stood tied along the locust hitch-rail opposite the brick courthouse.

Clear ditch water ran brisk beside the rough plank sidewalk on Water Street. Across the square two lean hogs rooted chestnut hulls under the brick pilasters of the portico while a deputy sheriff in mud-spattered boots tacked a stray-stock notice beside the double doors. Directly facing the courthouse stood a narrow two-story weatherboard house. Newly oiled horse-collars hung on pegs beside its lower doorway, sending the warm smell of bark-tanned sole leather, beeswax, and neat’s-foot oil clean across the street. Up the north gable climbed an unpainted outside pine stair to a four-panel door on the landing.

Joshua tied the claybank at the end of the locust rail and loosened her girth one hole. Harper tied his brown mare beside the roan of old Squire Wiseman, the Three Mile magistrate, knocked red Ledger dust off his trouser cuffs with his hat brim, and nodded across at the brick portico.

“Ten minutes yet till the bell,” Harper said. “Come inside the hall while I get a gourd of well water and ask young McKinney, the register’s clerk, whether my meadow deed got indexed.”

Cool lime-plaster shade met them inside the brick passage. Tax lists and administrator’s sale bills hung three deep on a slatted pine board beside the clerk’s door. Across the hall the register’s room stood open behind its waist-high oak rail, empty save for the freckled nineteen-year-old deputy clerk shelving mortgage bundles. On the high standing desk by the south window two heavy calfskin county volumes — *Deed Book C* and *Deed Book D* — still lay spread wide instead of racked in their slots. Between them sat a sheet of clean white blotting paper, black with reverse strokes of a fine steel pen that did not belong to the county inkpot.

Harper drank half a dipper from the cedar bucket by the door and leaned an elbow on the oak rail. “Register upstairs already, son?”

“Went across five minutes back, Mister Harper,” the boy said. He shut *Deed Book D* with both hands and slid it toward the rack. “Had me pulling Cranberry and Harrell’s township books since Tuesday morning for that revenue gentleman across the street.”

Harper set the tin dipper back on its nail without haste. His eyes met Joshua’s once across the oak rail, flat and expressionless, then looked out into the street glare.

“Door’s latched open upstairs,” was all Harper said.

They crossed Water Street and climbed the steep pine treads together.

Inside the upper room seven split-bottom chairs and a planed pine bench had been set in an even half-circle on freshly swept boards. No spittoon sat by the hearth. No tobacco stain marked the whitewashed baseboard. Under the front window stood a plain walnut table holding a cast-iron screw press for tissue letter-copies, a pewter inkstand with two clean steel pens, a locked tin dispatch box, and two thick sheep-bound volumes of the *Revised Statutes of the United States*. Narrow slips of white paper marked places along the fore-edge. Behind the washstand in the corner leaned a leather saddle-scabbard with the brass butt-plate of a blued lever carbine showing above the flap.

Five men already sat along the wall. Closest to the door sat the Mitchell County sheriff with both thumbs hooked in his vest pockets. Next to him sat the register of deeds holding a rolled linen township map across his knees, old Squire Wiseman from Three Mile, a gray magistrate from Grassy Creek, and the young timekeeper from the Cranberry bloomery.

Behind the walnut table stood a man of thirty-eight in a dark broadcloth frock coat brushed clean of stage clay. His dark hair was trimmed short above his ears. His linen cuffs showed spotless at the wrists. Wind and sun had browned his spare face, not desk lamp-smoke. When Joshua and Harper crossed the sill, he stepped two paces round the table corner without offering a politician’s handshake and inclined his head slightly from the shoulders.

“Good day, gentlemen,” he said. His voice sat middle-low, clear, and wholly unhurried, carrying neither Yankee clip nor mountain drawl. “Mister Harper, from the Cranberry forks. And Mister Joshua Brennan from Elk Cove. Pray take those two chairs by the window.”

Joshua sat down on the hickory slats. He laid his wool hat crown-up across his knees. Neither he nor Harper asked how a stranger ten days in the county had matched their faces to their creeks.

Down in the valley a church bell struck twelve slow iron strokes. When the twelfth ring died over the shingle roofs, the man stepped behind the walnut table, rested four fingers lightly on the closed sheep-bound volume, and looked directly at each face in the half-circle.

“My name is William Parrish,” he said. “I hold commission from Washington as an Internal Revenue Agent under Section thirty-one fifty-two of the Revised Statutes, assigned this autumn to the Sixth Collection District of North Carolina. I asked you up these stairs at noon so that no householder in your townships need learn the government’s business from road rumor or a marshal’s warrant.”

Old Squire Wiseman cleared his throat once. “We had a deputy collector up from Statesville two summers back, Mister Parrish. Took chicken dinner at the boarding house, rode three miles out Cane Creek, and went back down the mountain before frost.”

“Congress altered the service two Augusts ago, Squire,” Parrish said evenly. “The old district supervisors were abolished in seventy-six. Agents in my corps answer to no county party committee, and I do not touch one cent of public money across this table. All tax receipts pass by draft or stamp through Collector Mott’s office at Statesville. Every moiety and informer’s percentage was repealed six summers back. Ten seizures in Mitchell County or none between today and grass adds not a dime to my salary.”

He drew the upper white paper slip half an inch further from the sheep-bound volume without opening the board.

“Last spring,” Parrish went on, “Commissioner Raum authorized suspended sentences across the western districts for men making untaxed spirits who came into court, surrendered their worms, and gave bond to quit. Over three hundred men in these mountains took that mercy before wheat harvest. Half those furnaces were banked hot again before the September corn hardened. On the twentieth of August Washington shut that door for good.”

Down on Water Street an ox-wagon creaked past on dry hubs. Nobody in the half-circle moved a boot.

“The excise on distilled spirits stands where Congress fixed it three Marches ago,” Parrish said. “Ninety cents upon every proof gallon. Any citizen wishing to distill grain or apples in Mitchell County may do so under the law upon filing survey and bond before he lights wood. Because few farmers have read the Act of July, eighteen sixty-eight in the text, I will read two short provisions once so no man is misled.”

He opened the upper volume flat at the first slip. His gray eyes dropped to the fine print, reading at the flat, even pace of a clerk calling scale weights.

“Under Section eight, no collector may approve a distiller’s bond unless the distiller owns the land in fee simple, wholly unencumbered by any mortgage, judgment, or other lien. Where a mortgage or lien stands of record, the distiller must first file the written consent of the fee owner and of every mortgagee, expressly stipulating that the lien of the United States for taxes and forfeitures shall take priority over the mortgage.”

Joshua’s right thumb stayed still on his hat brim. Three chairs along the wall, the register of deeds looked down at the brass ferrule of his rolled map.

Parrish turned three leaves to the second white slip.

“Under Section twelve, no person shall use any still, boiler, or other vessel for distilling in any dwelling-house, or in any shed, yard, barn, or enclosure connected with a dwelling-house. Congress fixed the penalty at not less than five hundred dollars fine and imprisonment up to two years. Under Section forty-four, where untaxed spirits are run upon any tract, not only the copper, mash tubs, and work beasts on the ground are forfeited. All right, title, and interest in the lot or tract of land itself — and all premises connected therewith — are forfeited outright to the United States.”

He closed the thick sheep board without a sound.

“The autumn term of the United States Circuit Court at Statesville sits eleven days from today, on Monday, the twenty-first of October,” Parrish said. “No Cranberry Township case goes down to that term. Every assessment, every book examination, and every seizure made in these hills during the coming winter goes onto Judge Dick’s docket for the third Monday of April. That day is the twenty-first of April, eighteen seventy-nine. Six months and eleven days from noon today.”

---

Warm leather grease smelled up through the pine floor-cracks from the saddler’s bench underneath. For ten breaths nobody spoke.

Then the sheriff unhooked one thumb from his vest pocket and tilted his chair back against the whitewash. “Cove traces freeze hub-deep up Toe River by Christmas, Mister Parrish. My two boys stay rode down serving county executions and keeping order round the mica shafts. County taxpayers don’t pay us to hunt blockade whiskey in the laurel after dark.”

“I ask no county deputy to serve a federal writ, Sheriff,” Parrish answered pleasantly. “Deputy United States marshals draw federal mileage, and Statesville sends a sworn gauger whenever spirits are proved. Today I ask only plain township fact from men of standing. Does one bonded grain distillery stand registered on Cranberry or Elk waters?”

The register of deeds shook his head once. “Not one on my index since sixty-one.”

“Take the Indian corn crop up those coves, then,” Parrish said, turning half toward Wiseman and Harper. “Wagon haul over Jonas Ridge to Morganton takes two days down and two back. When valley merchants pay twenty-five cents the bushel, shelled corn eats its own worth in mule feed before it sees the cars. What consumes your crop?”

Squire Wiseman stroked his white chin-beard with three slow fingers. “Hogs take the bulk of it, Mister Parrish. Mountain hogs take hard corn six weeks before killing frost to set solid lard. Winter steers over on the balds eat the nubbins. Cold country up under Roan.”

“And our company teams,” the young bloomery timekeeper spoke up quickly, eyes on his bootlaces. “When the Forge hammers bar iron and hauls blooms over the gap to Elizabethton, twelve mules burn forty bushels every week.”

Parrish listened to each man with grave, unhurried courtesy. Not a line moved round his mouth. Picking up one steel pen, he dipped it shallow in the pewter stand and wrote two neat lines in a narrow canvas-bound pocket book beside the letter press.

“Where hard money is scarce,” Parrish observed calmly as his pen dried, “trade leaves as clear a track as silver. When Congress abolished the district supervisors, it transferred to agents in my service their authority under Section thirty-one sixty-three to summon any person and examine any merchant’s day book, freight bill, or store ledger touching commodities subject to tax. Ten gallons of spirits traded across a crossroads counter for bar iron or coffee sit as plain on paper as a bank draft.”

In the corner chair the bloomery timekeeper stopped swinging his foot.

Then Parrish’s gray eyes came across to the window.

“You keep the public gristmill at the head of Elk Cove, Mister Brennan?”

“One run of stones,” Joshua said.

“Water holds through hard frost?”

“Freezes nights in January,” Joshua said. “Turns by day once I bar the ice off the buckets.”

“North Carolina law still fixes public toll at the eighth dish?”

“One eighth on bolted bread meal,” Joshua said. “One fourteenth when a neighbor asks coarse chop for stock. Struck flat in a county-sealed half-bushel.”

Parrish gave a small, dry nod. “A fourteenth leaves heavier sacks going home than an eighth. Do Carter County wagons cross the line to buy meal at your door?”

“No wagon grade clears our gap,” Joshua said. “Men up my branch lay two bushels across a mule’s withers and ride home before dark.”

Parrish looked six inches left to Harper. “And down at the turnpike forks above the Forge, Mister Harper? Your township sheet sets down two hundred acres and a double-crib log barn beside the county road.”

“Timothy hay and forty winter ewes,” Harper said. Both broad hands rested flat on his knees. “My boys wagon salt and bar iron up from Hoss Tyler’s fork when the ground holds. Nothing stands under my oak shingles that calls for a revenue stamp, Mister Parrish.”

“Then no illicit distiller fires a furnace on Cranberry waters,” Parrish said quietly — neither sneering nor pressing, only setting the government’s two words square on the walnut board where the sheriff had laid the mountain word.

Nobody in the half-circle answered him. Squire Wiseman studied the brick courthouse cornice across the street. The Grassy Creek magistrate pared a thumbnail with a shut pocket-knife.

Parrish pressed a strip of clean blotter once over his two lines, closed the canvas book, and stood erect.

“I am obliged to you for riding twenty-two miles in October frost, gentlemen,” he said. “This room stands open every Tuesday and Thursday until spring whenever a landowner cares to read how the statute touches an adjoining line or an unpaid mortgage before trouble begins. Any man who climbs that stair on his own boots speaks with me alone, and no clerk sits behind the door.”

Chair legs scraped back over the swept pine. Men reached for their hats and stepped out onto the bright landing. Boots clumped single-file down the steep outside treads toward Water Street.

Joshua went out behind Harper’s wide back, last in the line. As Joshua’s palm took the unpainted rail post, Parrish spoke two paces inside the open doorway.

“Does the branch road above Mister Tyler’s store stay passable for a shod horse after the November rains, Mister Brennan?”

Joshua halted on the top tread and looked half back. Across the sunlit floor the two sheep-bound statute volumes lay squared within a hair of the walnut corner.

“Passable as far as my mill-dam,” Joshua said. “Steep red clay and loose slate beyond it.”

“Thank you, Mister Brennan,” Parrish said. He inclined his head once and turned back to his press table.

---

Below the wooden footbridge at the edge of town they watered both mares in Cane Creek where the current ran clear over flat gravel.

Joshua untied his homespun poke from the cantle, spread a dry square of sacking on a flat rock, and poured out two quarts of his own white corn for the claybank and two for Harper’s mare so neither man spent town silver at the livery stable. While the horses crunched steady in the cold afternoon sun, old Squire Wiseman splashed through the shallow ford on his roan mare heading west toward Three Mile.

Wiseman checked his rein one breath in mid-stream, clear creek water dripping bright off his mare’s fetlocks. “Used to be a man hid his worm in a laurel slick and worked out his fine on the road,” the old magistrate said to nobody in particular. “Hard thing to hide a bottom meadow under laurel.”

He touched two fingers to his hat brim and rode on up the bank. Neither Joshua nor Harper answered him. When the mares had licked the last white kernel off the sacking, Joshua shook the cloth clean, tied it behind his saddle, and led the way north out of the valley.

Dark overtook them three miles south of Ledger hill.

No moon had cleared the Black Mountains yet. Sharp winter stars crowded thick above the spruce line toward Roan. Down on their left the North Toe brawled loud over ledges where August drought had starved it to dry boulders. Six miles past the dark mica dumps neither man spoke above the steady *clink-scrape* of eight iron shoes climbing frozen gravel.

Only when the wide, pale frost-flat of the Old Fields of Toe dropped behind them and black hemlock timber shut out the stars along Cranberry Creek did William Harper check his mare to an easy walk.

“You watch his table while he read out that sixty-eight law?” Harper asked through the dark.

“Watched his hands,” Joshua said.

“Never looked down once when he called my two hundred acres or your stones on Elk Cove,” Harper said. Saddle leather creaked under his thigh. “You stood beside me at that counter before noon when young McKinney shut *Deed Book D*. That man spent Tuesday and Wednesday copying out every boundary oak, every married woman’s deed, and every recorded mortgage in Cranberry Township before he ever asked one soul who owned a copper kettle.”

Joshua rode twenty paces with the raw creek wind cold against his cheekbones. “Battered copper only brings old-metal price at a courthouse sale.”

“Aye,” Harper said, low in his chest. “Fifty pounds of sheet copper a boy can pack up a laurel slick on one mule in ten minutes. Eighty-six acres of mill-seat and two hundred of bottom meadow stay right where the Almighty laid them till a deputy marshal tacks paper to a gatepost. And if Hoss Tyler’s store books go down to Statesville under that other section he named, half the men between Toe River and the Tennessee line sit written down in Tyler’s own hand.”

Above Hoss Tyler’s dark storehouse they drew rein beside Harper’s tall double-crib barn. Inside the notched logs winter steers shifted warm in dry oat straw and blew heavy against the chinking.

“Walk down the hill tomorrow and tell Jacob word for word what that man read out of Section twelve,” Harper said, hand on his gate pin in the starlight. “A log barn forty paces below a woman’s kitchen step ain’t a brush shelter up Beech Creek.”

“I’ll tell him,” Joshua said.

“Good night, Joshua.”

“Good night, William.”

Two miles up the narrow cove trace the claybank mare stumbled once on frost-heaved ironstone, caught her footing, and carried him home by the sound of the branch water alone.

Past eleven o’clock Joshua led her into his dark mill-lot. He lifted the stiff saddle off her sweated back, hung it on its wooden peg, and rubbed her loins dry with two twists of clean corn shuck until the hair lay flat and cool under his palm. He poured the rest of her oats into the box and drove the oak bar home across the stable door.

Alone on the frost-stiff bank above his millpond, he checked the black water line by starlight. Twelve hours with the gate wedged shut had brought the pond level within an inch of the spill plank, four full grindings banked quiet against tomorrow’s toll.

Down across the dark race, beyond the forked white oak and the knee-deep granite corner-stone where Hoss Tyler’s four-hundred-dollar mortgage stopped, a thin red seam of pine-knot fire glowed steady again beneath the sill logs of Margaret’s lower barn. Once, clear through the cold air, came the dull wooden thump of an oak bung driven home into a barrel stave. Faint on the freezing draft rose the sweet, heavy smell of scalded corn mash forty paces from her dark kitchen door. Every thread of overflow slipping past Joshua’s shut gate ran down the split chestnut trough across the line to keep his brother’s copper coil cold.

Six months and eleven days stood between midnight and the third Monday of April at Statesville. Joshua turned up the steep path to his bed without looking down at the fire a second time.
