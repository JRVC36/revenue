## Chapter 5: Keep Making It

Cold sleet blew slantwise out of the Tennessee gap by two o’clock. Gray needles ticked steady against Joshua’s oilskin coat.

Under the low eaves of his drying shed Joshua unbuckled the wet leather saddlebags off the claybank mare. His knuckles were stiff and red from the two-mile climb out of Cranberry Creek. Before hanging the bridle on its locust peg, he reached inside his wool waistcoat, drew out his oilskin pouch, and checked the folded slip inside with a dry thumb.

Hoss Tyler’s brown ink had dried before noon: *Received of J. Brennan, twenty-four dollars lawful money, one year’s interest to November tenth, eighteen seventy-eight, upon deed of trust.*

Nothing else lay in the pouch. Thirty-six silver halves, four quarters, and the worn five-dollar United States note had gone into Tyler’s iron safe beneath the walnut desk, leaving Joshua’s cabin drawer empty of coin until spring wagons came back over the mountain. Beside Tyler’s notice-board at the forks, nailed fresh into the wet pine clapboards next to the Bakersville stage bill, two printed Treasury sheets had hung limp in the sleet, setting forth section forty-four in black government type for any teamster who could spell it out.

Joshua rubbed the mare’s chilled back dry with a twist of oat straw, latched the stall bar, and walked down the slick bench path past the forked white oak.

Gray sleet hissed into the race ditch. Across the squared granite cornerstone his cold pond water still ran swift down the two hollowed chestnut troughs into Margaret’s spring branch.

Forty paces below Margaret’s shut kitchen shutter, the door of her lower log barn stood propped six inches ajar with a split billet to let light across the sill. Inside, slow tap-strokes of a wooden mallet rang hollow on white oak.

Joshua halted under the dripping eaves. He knocked slush off his hat brim against his thigh.

Jacob knelt alone near the cold firebox in his patched wool roundabout, an eighty-gallon mash barrel upright between his knees. With a curved iron drawing knife he pared a hair’s shaving off the bevel of a seasoned white-oak stave, set it back into the gap of the circle, and tapped the top iron hoop down tight with a hickory driver. Neither Caleb nor Matthew was in the shed. Up on the ridge behind the corncrib an axe thudded dull and regular in the wet timber.

Jacob sighted down the stave seam without looking up. “Tyler take that worn five-dollar bill at full count?”

“Took it,” Joshua said. “Signed the back of the receipt before twelve.”

“Road slick below Harper’s?”

“Freezing in the shade,” Joshua said. He rested one wet boot on the threshold log without stepping inside. “Two printed revenue bills came up on the Saturday hack from Bakersville. Somebody tacked them beside Hoss’s door.”

Jacob drove the second iron hoop a quarter-inch lower. Rust flaked off the oak. “Let him tack paper till his thumb’s sore. Sleet’ll pulp it before Christmas.”

“It wasn’t paper I came down to tell you again, Jacob,” Joshua said. “When that man Parrish sat his bay horse up by our white oak two Saturdays back, he never craned his neck to peer under these eaves for your copper.”

Jacob’s mallet stopped on the hoop edge. He looked up.

“He looked at my cart ruts crossing the stone,” Joshua said. “He looked at those four locust stakes Caleb and I drove in August, and my ditch water running down your trough. And he counted the steps between Margaret’s kitchen porch and this sill.”

Jacob stood up slowly from the barrel, brushing white-oak curls off his knees. “Margaret’s forty acres don’t owe Hoss Tyler a copper cent. Father’s old Watauga grant came down clear through her people, and her Mitchell deed sits in the cedar box in her own name.”

“Section forty-four doesn’t ask whose debt is on the paper,” Joshua said. “It takes the ground the still stands on, and every premise connected with it.”

Jacob let out a short, rough breath through his nose. Turning to the wall bench, he laid the wooden mallet down beside his drawing knife. “What does his blessed government paper even cost down at Statesville, then, if a man wanted to buy their stamp and shut his mouth?”

“Ninety cents cash coin on every hundred-proof gallon before the bung goes in,” Joshua said. “Half again what Tyler allows you in salt and cloth. And you couldn’t sign their bond if you had a bushel basket of silver dollars on that bench.”

Jacob frowned. “Why not?”

“Because no collector can approve a distiller’s bond unless the ground stands clear in the distiller’s own name, or every man holding a deed or a mortgage on the connected premise signs a writing at the courthouse putting the United States ahead of his own lien,” Joshua said. “Go ask Hoss Tyler to sign away first call on my four hundred dollars so you can boil corn. And even if Hoss lost his wits and signed it, section twelve forbids any still inside a barn, shed, or yard connected with a dwelling house. A five-hundred-dollar fine and up to two years.”

Silence held under the low oak rafters. Outside, sleet ticked faster on the stacked firewood.

Jacob looked down at his callused knuckles. “Valley wagons don’t pay mule feed on shelled corn, Joshua. Four mouths in that house eat salt and wear cloth twelve months of the year. Father boiled his winter chop on this branch before Washington had a clerk in Statesville. I steal no man’s horse. I work my own grain with my own hands.”

“Right has nothing to do with it,” Joshua said. “They have guns and paper.”

Jacob looked past Joshua’s shoulder up the wet slope toward the invisible boundary stone under the laurel, then back into his elder brother’s eyes. Neither man raised his voice.

“Rafe Winters rode over from Sugar Hollow while you were down at the forks,” Jacob said. “Old Amos Councill’s walking across from North Fork after supper, and Boone Pritchard and the boys from Little Elk. Too raw to talk in the laurel. Ten or twelve of us mean to step into your millhouse round seven.”

Joshua’s face did not move. Up the bank stood his millhouse, dry-floored, thirty yards inside his mortgaged eighty-six acres.

“You barring your latch against neighbors?” Jacob asked.

Joshua looked once up toward Margaret’s closed kitchen shutter, where thin blue hickory smoke flattened under the sleet, then back at Jacob.

“I drop no bar on my door before nine,” Joshua said. “And I give no man leave to speak for my stones.”

He turned on the wet chips and climbed the path alone through the gathering dusk.

---

By seven o’clock hard sleet had crusted white along every fence rail. Wind rattled the oak clapboards of the millhouse.

One square tin lantern hung by a bent nail from the tie-beam, its lard-oil wick turned down to the breadth of a fingernail. Dim yellow light reached the hanging iron scale, four stacked sacks of oat hulls, and the worn oak rim of Joshua’s county-sealed twenty-eight-pound half-bushel tub upturned in the middle of the floor. Beyond that narrow ring the corners lay dark, smelling thick of wet sheep’s-wool coats, mutton tallow on heavy brogans, and cold corn dust.

Men came up the cove road singly and by twos without lanterns. Each man stamped crusted ice off his soles on the sill stone before pulling the heavy plank door shut behind him.

Rafe Winters from Sugar Hollow came in first, shaking sleet out of his thick red beard, forty winters old and broad enough in the shoulders to lift a full mash barrel out of a sled bed alone. Behind him walked Boone Pritchard and Eben Gouge from Little Elk, where a thirty-gallon copper kettle served three cousins’ cabins, followed by Larkin Dugger from Beech Creek and six quiet heads of cove households whose mules wore Joshua’s path smooth every autumn.

Then the latch lifted again, and old Amos Councill from North Fork stepped inside, past seventy, lean as a seasoned hickory rail in a homespun jeans coat patched at both elbows. His dead son’s widow Sarah kept five thin acres up the cold fork with fifteen-year-old Lish, the grandson who split locust fence pins along the ridge to earn their winter meal.

Right behind old Amos, filling the doorway, came William Harper from the turnpike farm, his wet wool hat in his hand and his two eldest grown sons, Luke and Nathan, quiet at his shoulders. Harper owned no copper and ran no chop. Nobody stopped him at the sill. Men nodded low to him and made room beside the oat bin.

Joshua stood back against the upright post of his sack scale, arms folded across his chest, six feet outside the circle. Across the floor Matthew leaned his shoulders against the dark garner boards, thumbs hooked inside his frayed belt, watching every pair of muddy boots cross the planks.

In the center of the lantern ring sat Caleb on the upturned half-bushel tub.

Across Caleb’s knees lay his long percussion rifle, forty-four caliber and heavy-barreled, kept clean since he was sixteen. Unhurriedly he drew a strip of oiled flannel on a hickory wiping stick down the bore, lifted it clear, and wiped a speck of sleet damp off the iron hammer lock with his thumb while older men talked around him.

“Man’s got one hired clerk and a stove over the saddler’s shop,” Rafe Winters said, slapping his wet mittens once against his knee. “Sheriff told Wiseman plain at October court he wouldn’t send a county deputy up Toe River after blockade whiskey if Washington paid gold. Let that Mister Parrish ride ten miles up Sugar Hollow in January mud. His bay horse’ll cast both foreshoes before he clears the ford.”

“Didn’t cast a shoe riding to Roaring Creek Thursday,” Boone Pritchard answered from the shadow by the hay ladder. “Took two hired men out of Bakersville before sunup and chopped Simp Mace’s little twenty-gallon boiler flat with a poll axe. Poured twelve barrels of sweet mash down the creek bank.”

“Simp Mace set his furnace fifty yards off the public bridle trace where any peddler smelled sour beer,” Winters shot back. “Hadn’t sense enough to plug his spout.”

Old Amos Councill leaned both knotted hands on his sourwood staff and cleared the phlegm from his throat. Men went quiet for his age.

“My grandfather’s uncle lived down on Catawba waters before Watauga was cut off,” old Amos said, his thin voice dry as corn shucks. “King’s tax sheriff rode up their creek once with a red paper in his hatband to distrain a widow’s bedstead and pewter dish. Four boys took the girth off his saddle at the ford, set him backward on a blind heifer, and led her across the county line without raising a welt on his skin. Never saw his coat again.”

Nobody laughed. Around the dim ring every man over forty looked down at the plank floor.

Amos Councill looked across the lantern light toward the scale post. “Joshua. You sat in that room at Bakersville with William here. Tell these boys straight what the man read out of his book.”

Joshua did not step forward from the post.

“Ninety cents in lawful money on every hundred-proof gallon,” Joshua said. “Nearly a full ten-hour day’s axe-hire on every single gallon in your jug. A five-hundred-dollar fine and up to two years at Statesville for any copper set in a barn or yard near a house. And under section forty-four, where untaxed whiskey runs on a deed, the government forfeits the kettle, the beasts, the tract of land itself, and every premise connected with it—cutting ahead of any man’s mortgage.”

Nobody spoke for five breaths. Sleet rattled sharp against the north shutter.

---

Down on the upturned tub Caleb withdrew the hickory rod from his rifle muzzle. Setting the butt plate gently against his wet boot toe, he drew the iron hammer back to the half-cock notch with his thumb.

*Click.*

In the quiet millhouse the small steel sound carried to every wall.

“One horse comes up through the rhododendron narrows below Hoss Tyler’s fork one breast wide,” Caleb said. His voice was low, level, and free of bluster, as if he were laying out how to fell a crooked oak clear of a fence. “High slate bank on the upper side, deep laurel over the creek on the lower. One man sits dry under the hemlock roots before Christmas. Bay horse goes back to Bakersville with an empty saddle, and that room above the saddler’s shop stays shut till June.”

Over against the garner boards Matthew’s back stiffened straight against the pine.

Jacob stepped one long stride into the lantern light until his boot stood six inches from Caleb’s knee.

“No,” Jacob said.

Caleb looked up calmly into Jacob’s face without taking his hand off the stock.

“Put that talk down under this roof and leave it buried,” Jacob said, hard enough to cut iron. “We’re no bushwhackers shooting a man out of his saddle from a laurel slick. Kill a federal officer on Cranberry Creek and Washington sends fifty marshals up this valley before the ground thaws and hangs three men at Statesville. Nobody points a rifle at a man on the county road. Hear me, Son?”

Caleb held Jacob’s eyes two seconds, then lowered his chin half an inch. “I hear you, Father.”

Jacob turned to the circle of wet coats. “Winter’s locking down. Move copper out into the open laurel in January and your mash freezes solid in the staves before it works. Keep what we’ve got dry under roof, burn seasoned hickory after dark so no black smoke hangs in the cove by day, and quit sledding jugs down to Tyler’s on Saturday afternoons when town riders pass.”

“Who watches the turnpike fork?” Eben Gouge asked.

“One boy up on Beech Knob from sunup till four,” Jacob said. “From that south rock a boy looks two miles clear down Yellow Mountain Turnpike past the Forge bridge. If strange shod horses turn off toward Elk Cove, two long blasts on a hollowed turkey-wing bone carry clean up both hollows in ten seconds. Gives any man twenty minutes to rake his fire dead and pitch hay over his cooling barrel.”

Old Amos Councill tapped his sourwood stick once on the plank. “Sarah’s boy Lish splits locust fence pins up on Beech Knob four days the week anyhow. Boy’s fifteen and sharp-eyed as a hawk. Half a bushel of white meal a week keeps his mother’s skillet greased through February.”

“Half a bushel out of Sugar Hollow and Little Elk turn about,” Rafe Winters said. “And from tonight till April court sits, no man asks whose mule carried coarse chop home, and no man knows whose sled went down the junction road after dark. What a man doesn’t know, a man can’t swear to at Statesville.”

Low nods went round the lantern ring. Men shifted weight, ready to reach for the door latch.

Beside the oat bin William Harper had not nodded.

Sixty next birthday, broad-backed in his gray homespun coat, Harper stepped half a pace clear of the doorpost so the small yellow flame caught his square-trimmed beard and the deep lines beside his eyes. His two hundred acres lay flat across the mouth of Elk Cove where every sled road met the county turnpike. Four sons stood behind his name, and not one copper rivet had ever crossed his sill.

“Jacob,” Harper said.

The shuffling boots stopped. Jacob turned toward the door.

“Don’t fire another kettle this winter, Jacob, or that man will take the roofs off men that never made a gallon.”

Dead quiet dropped across the room. Nobody coughed. Even the sleet outside sounded loud on the oak shingles.

Rafe Winters spat brown tobacco juice into the loose hull chaff beside the post. “Easy talk for a man with forty tons of cured timothy hay in a tight double-crib loft and four grown boys hauling Forge iron, William. My young ones don’t eat hay.”

Harper did not turn his head toward Winters. He stood looking straight at Jacob five breaths longer around the silent circle.

Nobody spoke. Jacob’s eyes stayed level and hard as flint on the floor between them.

Harper settled his damp wool hat on his head. Once he looked past the lantern to the scale post where Joshua stood silent with folded arms. A single brief nod passed from Harper’s chin to Joshua’s. Then Harper lifted the oak latch. With Luke and Nathan behind him, he stepped out into the sleet and pulled the door shut.

---

Nobody lingered long after that.

Men buttoned collars to the chin, settled wet hats low, and slipped out by ones and twos without shaking hands or writing a scratch on paper. Rafe Winters went up the north bank toward Sugar Hollow; old Amos Councill took Larkin Dugger’s arm across the slick stones toward the North Fork footpath.

At the doorway Caleb lifted the tin lantern off its nail and held the plank wide against the wind so light fell across the iced sill log.

“Watch that glaze on the lower step, Father,” Caleb said, rifle cradled in his dry left arm.

“Got it,” Jacob said. Side by side, their shoulders matched to the same steady mountain stride, the two of them walked down into the sleet toward Margaret’s dark forty acres.

Matthew came out of the garner shadow last.

He had not spoken one syllable the whole two hours. Turning his thin coat collar up around his ears, he stepped over the threshold without looking back toward the scale post where his uncle stood, and followed three paces behind Jacob and Caleb’s lantern down the muddy ruts. Within twenty yards the driving sleet swallowed the light.

Joshua slid the heavy oak bar across the inside of his mill door.

Alone under the rafters, he hung the spare lantern on the scale hook and took the flat hickory splint broom down from its peg behind the garner. Methodically, working from the far wall toward the race crack, he swept every frozen clod of red turnpike clay, every crushed oat hull, and Rafe Winters’s wet tobacco quid off the wide oak planks. Tomorrow morning widow women and old men like Elihu Burleson would lead mares up to this sill with half-bushels of bread corn. Nothing on Joshua’s floor would show that thirteen men had stood inside his mortgaged walls after dark agreeing not to know their neighbors’ business.

He leaned the broom back in its corner and cracked the side shutter an inch to shake the dust out over the ditch.

Icy wind cut across his knuckles. Down below in the pitch dark, forty yards down the slope where no lantern burned, sleek black pond water slid under a skin of sleet along Joshua’s race. Across the granite boundary stone it poured down the split-chestnut trough he had propped there with his own hands in August, vanishing under the eaves of Margaret’s barn.

Joshua hooked the iron shutter latch tight. Pinching the hot lard-oil wick between wet thumb and forefinger, he snuffed the fingernail flame into black dark.
