# Act One: The Threshold

## Chapter 1: The Water Is Right

Before sunup the creek came down out of the laurel cold enough to ache in the wrist when Joshua reached through the wooden trash rack to clear the wet birch leaves from the slats. He stood on the sill plank with the iron crowbar across his knee until the pond behind the crib dam took back the inch of head it had lost in the night.

Inside the millhouse he set the county half-bushel and the cedar peck tub beside the hopper. He wiped the meal dust from the toll dish with his thumb. North Carolina allowed a public miller the eighth part of Indian corn when he ground it fine for a woman’s bread tray, seven pounds out of the fifty-six. Chops took the fourteenth part when he lifted the runner stone on the bridge-tree and cracked the grain coarse. Four pounds to the bushel was hog feed in December. In late August, with the old cribs scraped to the floor boards and the new ears still milk-soft in the shuck, every man up Elk Cove who brought shelled corn before breakfast asked for chop. Nobody fattened hogs in August.

Joshua took the mill pick from the window ledge. He laid his palm flat on the cold granite of the bedstone before lowering the hoop and brushed a crust of yesterday’s meal from the master furrow so the draft would carry cool to the skirt. Water leaked in a thin silver ribbon past the shut headgate. Outside, drops ticked slow into the buckets of the overshot wheel. Enough water stood in the pond for four turns before noon if he minded the gate. By September, if the dry spell held across the state-line ridge, a man bringing three bushels after dinner would wait on the bench until the creek filled the dam again.

Boot heels struck the plank bridge below the tailrace. Joshua hung the toll dish on its peg. He laid his hand on the iron lighter-screw.

Two mules came up out of the alders along the cart road that climbed from his brother’s wife’s flat, their hames clinking dry in the fog and four two-bushel tow sacks tied across the pack saddles with hickory bark. Caleb walked at the mare’s shoulder, an ash stick under his arm. His hat sat level over his eyes. Before stepping into the mill yard he looked once up the empty wagon trace toward the gap. Behind the bay, Matthew switched pokeweed tops with a peeled willow wand.

Caleb halted the lead mare at the loading sill. He loosed the bark tie with two turns of his wrist. “Water up?”

“Up an inch since midnight,” Joshua said.

“Enough to run four sacks?”

“Enough if I don’t grind flour.”

Caleb swung the first sack from the saddle tree onto his shoulder, carried it across the puncheon floor without setting his boot twice in the spilled meal, and stood it upright beside the hopper. “Chop.”

Matthew dragged the second sack off the bay mule’s hip. The corner dropped hard on the sill. He blew chaff off his lip. “Mother wants a half-peck out of the last bag bolted fine if the cloth ain’t torn, Uncle Joshua, on account of we’re down to the bottom of the gum-tree chest.”

“Bring the clean sack last, then,” Joshua said. “I won’t sweep the curb twice.”

Caleb went back for the third load. At the bay mule’s flank he stopped before touching the rope. With two fingers he lifted the leather breeching strap where the buckle tongue had skipped a hole and rubbed raw hair above the hock, drew it one notch tighter, and tucked the tail clean through the keeper. Matthew stayed inside the door. His eyes followed the brass poise on Joshua’s steelyard beam.

Joshua hooked the iron ring into the ears of the first sack. He slid the pea along the notched bar until the tongue stood level in the shears. “One hundred and twelve pounds less two for the tow,” he said. “Dry corn.”

“Father shelled from the back crib by the laurel,” Matthew said. His eyes stayed on the notches of the bar while his thumb worked against his breeches seam. “Down at Morganton last month the wagons got twenty-five cents the bushel when they got cash at all. Four sacks is eight bushels. That’s two dollars for hauling two days each way over Jonas Ridge and feeding two mules out of the load.”

Joshua unhooked the sack. He set his wrench on the lighter-screw. “You haven’t hauled to Morganton since spring.”

Matthew shut his mouth and took the other corner of the sack. Together they hoisted it breast-high and poured the yellow kernels into the hopper until the pyramid rose smooth under the damsel spindle.

Joshua gave the lighter-screw half a turn to the right, lifting the upper stone off the bed by the thickness of a sole-leather washer. He stepped to the lever post and drew the gate rod. Outside the wall the chute filled with a rush. Water took the buckets. The oak shaft groaned once in its greased gudgeons, and the pit wheel engaged the wallower with a dry wooden thump that settled into an even beat through the soles of their boots. The iron damsel knocked three times to every turn of the spindle. Shelled corn rattled down the shoe into the eye.

No flour fogged up around the hoop. Out of the meal spout came cracked flinty grit, each kernel split into three or four sharp pieces with the hull whole enough to drain clean in a mash tub and never paste to hot copper.

Nobody in the room said so. Caleb stood by the north window where the road from Hoss Tyler’s fork showed white for fifty yards between the chestnuts before bending into timber. His hands rested quiet on the ash stick. Matthew squatted by the meal trough. He caught a handful of warm chop from the spout and let it run back between his knuckles.

When the third sack emptied, Joshua pushed the gate rod halfway home. The stones slowed to a crawl. With the sealed four-pound toll scoop he dipped into the trough and struck the top level with a straight hickory stick. Six level scoops went into the ark for six bushels of chop. Twenty-four pounds. Bread meal at the eighth would have taken forty-two.

For the fourth sack Joshua turned the lighter-screw back down until the stones whispered edge on edge. He ran off a clean half-peck through the bolting reel for Margaret’s bread tray, then lifted the runner again for the rest of the bag. Caleb tied the ears of the chop sacks with twine. Joshua stepped to the pine tally board behind the door. Under Jacob’s cut mark he chalked seven bushels and a half of chop and half a peck of bolted meal.

“Tell your father the race is running full till sundown,” Joshua said. He wiped chalk from his thumb onto his apron. “I’ll shut the gate at dark to catch head for tomorrow.”

“He’s down at the lower barn since daylight,” Caleb said. He shouldered two sacks at once, one across his neck and the other under his elbow. When Matthew had tucked the bread poke dry under the saddle blanket, Caleb led the mules out across the bridge.

Joshua stood in the door until the alders hid them where the single cart road dropped off his hill toward Margaret’s fence line. Then he opened the drawer beneath the tally desk. He dipped his steel pen, copied the chalk line into his mill book in ink, and dusted dry sand from the saucer across the wet figure before shutting the lid.

---

After dinner the sun stood clear of Sugar Hollow. Behind the closed gate the millpond lay slack. Joshua weighed out fifty pounds of his own white bread meal from the bolted chest, tied the mouth of a clean linen sack, and carried it down the cart road on his shoulder.

The track followed his tailrace down the bench of the hill. On his left the ditched water ran quick over laurel roots and flat slate where his father had wedged stone thirty years back to keep winter freshets out of the wheel pit. On his right stood six acres of late corn. Its paper lay in Hoss Tyler’s safe against four hundred dollars owing. Halfway down the grade stood a forked white oak with three old surveyor’s chops healed black in the bark, and beside its root a squared granite stone set knee-deep in the turf.

Joshua stepped past the stone without breaking stride. Below that post the forty acres were Margaret’s, out of her father’s old Watauga tract, unmortgaged and clear of any man’s store book. The cart ruts went straight across the line without a gate. Beside them the tailrace dropped through a split chestnut trough over the bank to join the cold spring branch behind her lower barn.

Margaret stepped onto the dogtrot planks with a sedge broom in hand. An iron spider cooled by the doorstone. Her faded indigo apron was pinned neat at the bib, her hair braided tight off her temples against the August heat. At the sight of the linen sack on his shoulder she set the broom against the logs. She pushed the kitchen door wide with her foot.

“You didn’t have to bolt a whole fifty pounds,” she said. “Matthew brought the half-peck before nine.”

“Half a peck won’t see two grown boys past Sunday,” Joshua said. He set the sack on the scoured bench inside the cool kitchen. He untied the string.

Margaret dipped two fingers into the warm meal, rubbed a pinch against her thumb to try the grit, and nodded once before drawing the string tight again. “White corn grinds sweeter than that old yellow crib,” she said. “We’re down to two quarts of coarse salt in the gourd, Joshua, and no saleratus left worth naming. If you’re going down to Tyler’s with Jacob before sundown, tell him four yards of unbleached shirting if Tyler has the heavy weave, and leave it if it’s that thin Knoxville cloth you can read the almanac through.”

“I need a bar of flat iron for the wheel shroud anyway,” Joshua said. “I’ll look at the bolt myself.”

She glanced through the open dogtrot toward the bottom of the clearing. Forty paces below the kitchen step, where the alder thicket closed round the spring branch, the log barn stood with its loft door propped open a hand’s breadth on a cob. Clear heat shimmered without smoke above the shakes of the shed-room. Two split-oak buckets dried upside down on the wash bench.

“He ate cold middling at noon standing up,” Margaret said. “He’s doubling since eleven.”

Joshua took his hat from the peg and walked down the path between her pole beans and the late cabbages. Every row was hoed clean to the red clay, the trenches cut on the bias so a cloudburst off the ridge ran round the hills instead of washing them. Behind the barn the same hand showed in the stone-lined ditch that carried the spring tailing into a bed of tall sweet-flag without leaving a sour puddle for yellowjackets.

Inside the shed-room the air smelled of scalded meal, wet locust wood, and hot copper.

Joshua stopped on the threshold with one hand on the hewn jamb. He did not go in. Under the low rafters Jacob knelt on a folded tow sack beside the fieldstone furnace, feeding dry peeled hickory sticks two at a time beneath the belly of an eighty-gallon copper kettle so the flame stayed pale blue and never licked soot up the rivets. Over the collar of the pot the round cap sat luted tight with red clay kneaded into rye paste. From its crown the tapered copper arm ran five feet across the earthen floor into a locust-stave worm tub fed at the rim by a bark spout off the laurel spring.

At the foot of the tub a clear thread of spirit ran off the lip of the tail-pipe. It passed through washed flannel laid across a hickory funnel into a two-gallon stoneware jug.

Jacob did not turn his head. His thumb pressed wet clay over a pinhole hiss under the cap. Steam died under the callus. Only then did he wipe his hands on his knees, stand up out of the heat, and lay two fingers in the overflow trough where the cold spring water met the ditch from Joshua’s race.

“Stayed cold all day,” Jacob said.

“Pond held up better than Tuesday,” Joshua said from the doorway. “Caleb brought four sacks.”

“Seven bushels and a half,” Jacob said, naming the chop weight before Joshua gave it. He lifted a small glass proofing phial from a shelf peg, caught an inch of the clear stream from the tail-pipe, clapped his thumb over the mouth, and struck the bottom hard against his palm. Small round bubbles rose clean along the surface. Half sat above the liquor line and half below.

Jacob held the phial an inch toward the doorway light. The ring held steady without bursting. He poured the sample back through the flannel into the jug. “First two jugs came off before dinner,” he said, nodding toward the cool corner by the sill where two stoppered brown-glazed jugs sat sweating in damp oats. “Running slow now. Tailings by four.”

Joshua looked past the worm tub to the square hole sawed low in the back logs where the wooden trough carried the cold spring straight off Margaret’s bank and spilled the warm top-water into the sweet-flag ditch. Neither road dust nor creek mud reached here. From the wagon trace up the ridge, nothing showed below the chestnuts except barn shakes, bean poles, and two cows switching flies under a walnut tree.

“The water is right,” Jacob said.

Joshua turned his eyes once up the slope past the granite corner-stone toward the gray roof of his own millhouse, where the mortgage ran to the road and stopped at that stone.

“Margaret wants heavy shirting,” Joshua said, “and coarse salt and saleratus. Matthew got the sled hitched?”

“Out by the crib,” Jacob said. He drove a peeled corncob snug into the mouth of a third jug with his palm. Then he reached for dry fodder blades to bed them in. “Tell Matthew two jugs, no more. Tyler’s cask was half full Friday.”

---

Two miles down the branch trace to the Yellow Mountain Turnpike the sled runners slid slick over damp pine straw and grated shrill across every ledge of ironstone. Matthew walked at the mule’s cheek, the rope looped on his wrist. On the steep pitches he held the sled back with his hip so the two jugs nested in corn blades never knocked shoulders. Jacob walked behind the tailboard in a clean hickory shirt with the empty salt poke over his belt. Joshua kept to the dry bank above the ruts.

Where the cove road met the turnpike clearing above Cranberry Creek, William Harper and two of his sons had a wagon drawn alongside his double-crib log barn. The older boy pitched cured timothy through the wide loft doors while the younger raked scatterings off the tight oak floor inside. Harper lowered his three-tined fork when the sled drew even. He rested both wrists on the ash handle.

“Low water up your way, Joshua?” Harper called across the ditch.

“Grinding by catches till it rains,” Joshua said.

Harper looked once at the fodder bundle lashed tight in the middle of the narrow sled bed, then up at the dry yellow haze lying flat along the balsam line on Roan. He said nothing about the sled. “Creek down at the Forge won’t turn the trip-hammer past two o’clock,” he said. “Tell Tyler my boy’ll fetch our salt barrel Saturday.”

Jacob raised two fingers without halting the mule. They followed the red-iron ruts south toward the forks. Three miles down the creek lay the Cranberry Forge post office and the old ore banks where teams hauled black magnetic rock out by wagon whenever northern buyers sent an order. Here at the junction stood only Hoss Tyler’s long clapboard storehouse with its split-log porch, an iron tar kettle by the rail, and two saddled mares switching tails under the locusts.

The long room smelled of salt pork, green coffee, leather, and tar.

Hoss Tyler stood behind the high pine counter in his ticking apron with steel-rimmed spectacles pushed up into his thin gray hair, weighing tenpenny nails onto brown paper for an old man from North Fork Hollow. Years on Toe River had dried old Tyler to bone and tendon. His back stayed straight as a yardstick. He knotted the nail parcel with hemp twine, wrote three words in his ledger before pushing the nails across the wood, and waited until the North Fork man had gone down the steps before looking out at Jacob’s sled.

“Bring them round to the back room if there’s riders coming,” Tyler said.

“Road’s empty to Harper’s,” Jacob said. Matthew came up the steps carrying one jug in each fist. He set them quiet beside the cheese box.

Tyler pulled the cob from the first jug with his teeth. He spat a fleck of pith into the sawdust and tipped two fingers of spirit into his tall gauging glass. One rap on his palm brought the bead round at the line. Without tasting it he poured the sample back, drove the cob home with a wooden bung-starter, and proved the second jug the same way before setting both behind the salt barrels.

Over in the corner stood an empty forty-gallon barrel from a Salisbury rectifier, its red head chalk-smeared and a torn revenue stamp glued gray across the bung stave, holding axe helves and hickory hoe handles now. Tyler hooked a thumb toward it and wiped his ticking apron.

“Drummer out of Morganton offered me stamped Salisbury barrel goods Tuesday at a dollar eighty in silver,” Tyler said. “Smelled of fusel and scorched stave-tar through the bung. Good blockade whiskey pays its own freight on a mule, and a man knows whose corn went in it.”

Jacob did not smile. “Coarse Kanawha salt, fifty pounds,” he said. “Four pounds of green Rio coffee. Half a pound of saleratus. And Margaret says four yards of unbleached shirting if it’s the heavy Piedmont weave, none if it’s the thin.”

“Heavy bolt came off the Elizabethton wagon Thursday,” Tyler said. Taking the brass-tipped yardstick from its cleat on the counter edge, he unrolled the coarse cream web four lengths thumb to brass nail and nicked the selvage with his pocketknife. He tore the cloth straight across with a sharp dry rip. Over by the stove Matthew looked up from the boot peg.

While Tyler scooped green coffee onto the brass scale pan, Joshua walked back to the iron rack behind the stove. He lifted a six-foot bar of three-quarter-inch Cranberry flat iron forged at the creek hearth and sighted along its edge toward the door light. It ran true. “Put that bar to the mill,” he said.

Tyler pulled his spectacles down onto his nose and stepped to the sloped walnut desk where his bound calfskin day book lay open beside a stoneware inkstand. Every leaf carried its folio number in brown ink at the upper corner in Tyler’s upright hand, no blotches, no erasures, and no credit trusted to a man’s head past sundown.

First he turned to Jacob’s folio. On the right-hand side he entered four gallons at two dollars and fifty cents in trade, then wrote the salt, coffee, saleratus, and four yards of shirting line by line beneath the date until nineteen cents stood over to Jacob’s credit for October. Next he flipped back twelve leaves to Joshua’s mill folio. There he set down eighteen pounds of Cranberry bar iron against autumn grinding toll on Tyler’s own bread corn.

Before shutting the book Tyler turned three leaves toward the back to lay blotting paper across a damp line.

Matthew had come three steps closer from the stove, where a pair of new factory-pegged calfskin boots hung by their pull-straps from a rafter nail with the price penciled white on the sole. Under Tyler’s wrist lay Matthew’s own short page from the winter before: the Bakersville doctor’s night ride up Elk Cove in December when Margaret’s chest took cold, one chilled cast plow point in April, and twelve dollars still standing on the bay mule’s half-share.

Matthew’s lips moved once without sound. His eyes went from the boot sole to Tyler’s open page.

Joshua set the butt of the iron bar hard on the boards. At the ring of the iron Matthew looked off the open page. He stepped to the counter and lifted the fifty-pound salt sack onto his shoulder before his father reached for it.

“Frost inside three weeks if this wind stays north,” Tyler said. He shut the calfskin covers and slipped the clasp over the tongue. “Tell Margaret the soda’s fresh.”

---

Dusk came down blue out of the balsam gaps before the sled cleared the alders below Joshua’s dam. At the white-oak line Jacob and Matthew turned off toward Margaret’s barn with the salt and cloth while the first bullbats banked low over the slack pond.

Joshua carried the bar of flat iron into the millhouse, stood it behind the meal chest, and dropped the oak bar across the road door. Alone in the room, he struck a match on the stone hearth of the warming chimney and lit the wick of the lard-oil lamp on his tally desk. Yellow light climbed the chalked door. Long shadows from the steelyard hooks fell across the puncheon floor.

He sat on the three-legged stool, drew his ledger under the lamp, and checked the day’s ink line before turning to the back leaf where his cash engagements stood alone.

Everything in Elk Cove ran round a circle small enough for one mule to walk between breakfast and dark so long as it stayed in meal and jugs and iron. Eight bushels of shelled corn hauled two days over Jonas Ridge to Morganton brought two dollars in fractional silver when the town buyer bought at all and the team ate fodder carried from home. Cracked coarse under Joshua’s lifted stone and doubled slow in Jacob’s copper on Margaret’s forty acres, those eight bushels made twenty gallons of clear hundred-proof spirit. Across Hoss Tyler’s counter that brought twelve dollars and fifty cents in salt, coffee, cloth, and forged iron without a single United States note crossing Cranberry Creek.

On the back leaf of Joshua’s book stood the tenth of November and the year’s interest on his mortgage: twenty-four dollars in lawful money due at Bakersville on the four hundred owing against the mill, the dam, the corn, and the road. No jug paid that line. Every bushel of his own eighth-part meal toll between August and frost had to go out by wagon for coin to meet it, while down the hill his brother stood unencumbered on a wife’s forty acres and owed no man with a safe.

Joshua wiped his pen nib on a strip of wool, shut the ledger into the drawer, and pinched out the wick between wet thumb and forefinger.

Before going up the path to his cabin he unbarred the side door and stepped out onto the damp sill planks above the wheel to shut the headgate tight. Stars hung small and cold above the hemlocks on the Tennessee ridge. Down the slope, forty paces below Margaret’s dark kitchen window, a thin red seam of pine-knot fire lay steady under the sill logs of her lower barn, and the last overflow spilling under Joshua’s gate ran down the stone race straight into his brother’s worm tub.

Joshua pulled the wooden pin from its cleat. The gate plank dropped hard into its groove. Water went quiet in the chute, and he went inside.
