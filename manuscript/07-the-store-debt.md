## Chapter 7: The Store Debt

Steel rang short and flat against gray stone in the open mill loft. Outside the shutter, three days of warm March rain had turned Elk Cove Creek brown as steeped bark and sent six inches of white spill roaring over Joshua’s dam lip.

Joshua knelt on a folded meal sack beside the upturned runner stone. Every ten strokes he shifted his flat two-pound steel pick a thumb’s breadth nearer the eye. Crisp sparks winked blue at each blow. Half a winter of grinding flint corn had worn the shallow diagonal channels slick across the hard grit-stone until kernels mashed warm instead of shearing clean. Score those edges sharp before spring plowing, and one water-turn cut twice the meal without heating the flour.

Across the four-foot circle knelt nineteen-year-old Matthew, Jacob and Margaret’s younger boy. With a stiff wild-turkey wing in his right fist, Matthew swept white stone-dust out of each fresh-pecked furrow onto a spread tow-cloth.

Neither man spoke for twenty minutes. Steel ticked sharp on grit. Outside, brown creek water thundered down the sluice.

At four o’clock Joshua tested the inner rim with his thumb. Sharp stone teeth bit clean against his callus. He laid the steel pick in its wooden box and stood up, stretching the ache from his fifty-year-old back.

“Clear the cloth,” Joshua said. “She’ll cut cool till harvest.”

Together they gripped the iron crane-bail. Slowly they swung the twelve-hundred-pound stone level on its wrought-iron screw and seated it over the upright spindle without chipping an edge. Joshua hung the heavy wrench on its wall peg. When he turned to wash the white stone-powder off his wrists in the bucket, Matthew had not reached for his meal poke.

Matthew sat low on the oak doorsill above the ankle-deep red mud. He had pulled a flat hickory splinter from the kindling basket. Welt by welt, the boy scraped every speck of gray stone-dust and dried clay out of his greased Sunday brogans.

Joshua dried his hands on clean sacking. Nobody wore stitched Sunday leather to peck millstones on a muddy Monday afternoon.

Matthew pulled an oiled wool rag from his coat pocket. He rubbed both boot toes until dull tallow shone dark in the gray light. Twice his lips moved without sound, counting figures against the empty air. When Joshua’s boot creaked on the floorboard behind him, Matthew’s mouth shut tight. Back went the rag into his pocket.

“Seven weeks till April court,” Joshua said evenly. He banked his small hearth coal under wood ash. “Your father and Caleb splitting rails up Beech Creek tomorrow?”

Matthew stood up off the sill and brushed his trouser knees clean. Out at the hitch-post stood his young bay mule under its worn leather saddle, mud dried red to the fetlocks.

“Caleb’s riding up Beech Creek an hour before light,” Matthew said. He kept his eyes on the girth strap while drawing the buckle one hole tighter. “Two of Father’s yearling heifers broke fence above Dugger’s gap during the rain. Father’s staying round the house till noon to wedge axe-helves.”

Joshua stepped onto the porch and looked down the rutted hollow trace toward the forked white oak on the boundary line. At midnight Saturday Jacob had raked the last live coals from beneath the eighty-gallon kettle inside Margaret’s lower barn and set the copper cap off onto the hay-mow floor. Since then Joshua’s two twelve-foot chestnut troughs had lain turned bottom-up on dry stones beside the granite marker. Not a jug of Saturday’s run remained on Margaret’s forty acres. Every drop of pond water ran straight down Joshua’s own millrace again.

Matthew looped the hemp halter-rein once across his palm. Still he lingered beside the mule’s shoulder.

“Uncle Joshua,” the boy asked lightly, “what do store clerks over in Johnson City pay a week for table-board at the town boardinghouses?”

Joshua’s eyes came round from the race ditch to his nephew’s clean boots. “Three silver dollars a week took half a clerk’s wages when I wagoned flour across State Line Gap before the war,” Joshua said. “Town merchants hire their own cousins behind counters. Country boy without a trade sweeps livery stalls for cold cornbread and sleeps in the hayloft.”

Red crept up the boy’s thin neck above his collar button. Matthew looked away down the creek.

“Hoss Tyler sent word Saturday he wanted to check a loose nail in this mule’s off-fore shoe before spring plowing,” Matthew said quickly. “Thought I’d ride down to the forks before supper and let him set a clinch.”

Joshua glanced once at the bay mule’s off-fore hoof on the flat wet sill-stone. Thick red clay packed the iron rim tight. Every turned nail-clinch sat flat and sound against the horn wall.

Joshua did not point at the hoof. “Tell your mother when you come back up,” he said, “that I’m leading two bags of fine bolted meal down to Hoss’s back scale-room before six tomorrow morning for the early Cranberry Forge wagon.”

“I’ll tell her,” Matthew said.

Matthew swung into the saddle and turned the bay mule down the steep red-mud ruts through the dripping rhododendron laurel without looking back. Soon the splash of iron shoes faded below the boundary oak.

---

Cold blue dusk settled fast out of the high gaps above Roan Mountain. As the sun dropped behind the balsam ridge, raw frost came creeping back over the thawed clay, stiffening every hoof-track along the road into crusted iron.

Alone inside the darkening millhouse, Joshua took the flat birch broom from behind the hopper. He swept the last white grit off the pine curb into his iron pan. Out the race window went the dust.

He reached to drop the heavy pine lid of the dry grain chest. His hand stopped on the board.

Folded neat across the back corner lay Matthew’s heavy double-knit winter mittens, dyed dark walnut-brown in Margaret’s pot last autumn.

Joshua lifted both wool mittens in one broad palm. Outside the window, white frost already silvered the wet oak shingles of the wheel-shed. A mountain boy riding two miles down to the river turnpike at sundown and coming back up Elk Cove after dark never left thick wool mittens behind on a grain chest when March air bit raw against iron stirrups.

Seven weeks to the day stood between tonight and the third Monday of April at Statesville. Six weeks back, on Tuesday the twenty-eighth of January, a revenue clerk from Bakersville had laid Hoss Tyler’s two calfskin ledgers back on the walnut desk unblotted and untorn, keeping Parrish’s ten-day statutory receipt to the hour. Ten winter nights those two books had lain open under a brass lamp in town. Joshua’s mill page sat near the middle; Matthew’s nineteen dollars and forty cents stood near the back.

Creek water roared steady over the dam outside. Joshua stood still beside the chest. Slowly he laid both walnut-dyed mittens side by side on the window-ledge near the oak door-bar, where hands coming home in the dark would touch them first. He pinched out his tallow dip between thumb and finger. Down dropped the heavy bar into its iron hooks.

At four o’clock the next morning Matthew’s mittens still lay untouched on the frosty sill.

---

Thick white river-fog filled Elk Cove from ridge to ridge before daybreak on Tuesday, the fourth of March. Wet hemlock needles dripped slow onto dead oak leaves. Fog hid the road three strides past a mare’s ears.

Joshua walked ahead of his claybank mare down the slick red ruts with two fingers hooked inside her cheek-strap. Across her wooden pack-saddle rode two tight-sewn two-bushel bags of fine bolted flour, two hundred and twenty-four pounds of clean bread meal that took four strong arms to swing up on the tree. Hoss Tyler wanted both bags in his bin before the Cranberry Forge iron-wagon rolled past at half-past six.

At the forked white oak by the corner-stone Joshua slowed half a step. Forty yards down the wet grass slope to his right stood Margaret’s cabin and her father’s lower log barn, dim as gray boulders in the mist. No woodsmoke rose yet from the stone kitchen chimney. Down at the log barn both heavy doors hung shut and cold. High overhead to the left, the rocky nose of Beech Knob sat buried blind inside low cloud; fifteen-year-old Lish Councill never climbed up from North Fork to split locust pins until six-thirty, when day reached the turnpike ruts.

Joshua led the mare on through the silent rhododendron tunnel.

Where Elk Cove opened onto the broad river meadow at the Yellow Mountain Turnpike stood William Harper’s tight farmstead. No candle burned behind Harper’s kitchen shutters. Inside the long double-crib log barn beside the junction fence, wintered cattle shifted warm on dry straw and blew slow breaths through the chinks.

Half a mile south at Cranberry Forks, the long clapboard roof of Hosea Tyler’s storehouse loomed gray out of the fog. One narrow thread of yellow lamplight shone round the front window-shutter. Not a horse stood tied at the turnpike rail.

Joshua kept off the front gravel to leave the Forge teamsters room. Around the north clapboard gable he led the mare toward the rear meal-shed and log corn-crib.

Ten paces behind the crib wall his boots stopped in the wet weeds.

Tied short on a double hitch to a low cedar post behind Tyler’s corn-crib, squarely out of sight of any traveler on the turnpike, stood Matthew’s young bay mule.

Cold night fog stood thick as tallow beads across the empty leather saddle-seat. Both iron stirrups hung crossed neat over the wooden horn. When the claybank mare blew softly through her nostrils, the bay mule turned its ears once and shivered under frost-damped hair.

Joshua let go his mare’s strap. He stepped through the wet burdock and ran one bare thumb down the bay mule’s off-fore hoof. Every iron nail-clinch sat tight and cold. That mule had stood tied behind Tyler’s crib long before midnight.

Joshua led his mare to the wide rear plank door of the scale-room. Twice he rapped with his knuckles.

Heavy iron bolts slid back inside. Hoss Tyler pulled the rear door half open, holding a shaded tin candle-lantern low in his fist. At sixty-three the old teamster wore his thick wool blanket-coat buttoned to the chin over his striped ticking apron, gray hair combed flat with water.

“Early on the mud, Joshua,” Tyler said in his ordinary flat voice. He stepped back to clear the hanging iron scale-beam. “Set them on the low skid. Forge team’s running late in this fog.”

Joshua untied the rawhide pack-rope. He eased the first hundred-and-twelve-pound bag off onto his shoulder and set it upright on the pine scale-board. Bringing in the second bag, he pulled the rear crib door nearly shut against the wet draft. No lamp burned in the windowless scale-room. Only one thin bar of yellow light fell across the bins through the narrow counting-house doorway and the two-foot tally-hatch behind Tyler’s walnut desk.

“Matthew inside, Hoss?” Joshua asked quietly, brushing white flour off his sleeve. “His bay mule’s tied cold behind your corn-crib.”

Tyler slid the brass weight out along the iron scale-bar without dropping his voice. “Tapped at my side porch steps fifteen minutes back,” Tyler said plainly. “Asked leave to stand out the wet under my side eaves till six. Said a party riding up from Bakersville owed cash coin to clear his winter page on my book. Man brings silver to pay a winter balance, I open my counter at six.”

Joshua’s hand dropped from his sleeve. He took one step toward the partition doorway to walk out onto the side porch.

Out on the front turnpike gravel, shod horses crunched muffled out of the fog and halted. Curb-chains chinked soft in the wet dark. Two horses stopped right at the front porch rail, while farther north up the misty road other iron shoes thudded slow past the mouth of Elk Cove toward Harper’s meadow.

Hoss Tyler unhooked his tin lantern. Straight through the narrow partition doorway he stepped behind his slant-top walnut desk and turned up his brass counter lamp. Behind his broad wool back the plank partition door stood six inches ajar beside the open tally-hatch.

Joshua halted dead in the unlit scale-room. His palm rested flat on the cold pine rim of the flour chest, six feet behind Tyler’s elbow. Neither the front door nor the side porch door looked past the high walnut desk into the dark shed behind the scales.

---

The front iron thumb-latch clicked. Cold river-fog rolled low across the sawdust floor.

William Parrish stepped inside wearing his long dark riding cape beaded silver with mist. He lifted his slouch hat an inch from his neat brown hair and wiped his muddy riding boots once on the braided shuck mat. One pace behind him entered a tall Deputy United States Marshal in a belted frieze overcoat, brass star pinned inside his lapel and a leather warrant-case strapped at his hip. Silently the Deputy Marshal shut the front door against the fog and stood beside the bolt.

“Good morning, Mister Tyler,” Parrish said evenly.

“Morning,” Tyler answered from behind his walnut desk.

Parrish glanced once toward the side glass. Boots scraped wood outside under the eaves. Halfway open swung the side door, and Matthew stepped across the sill.

The boy wore his scraped, tallow-rubbed Sunday brogans and his buttoned homespun coat. He stood bareheaded in the cold room with his wet wool cap crushed tight in both red hands against his belt. He did not raise his eyes to Tyler’s face and never looked toward the dark scale-hatch behind the desk.

No one in the room wasted a word on weather.

“Folio one hundred and fourteen in your current ledger, if you please, Mister Tyler,” Parrish said quietly, unbuttoning one wet leather glove. “The account of Matthew Brennan.”

Hoss Tyler slid his steel-rimmed spectacles onto his nose. Beside the stoneware inkwell lay the two heavy calfskin store books brought back clean from Bakersville on the twenty-eighth of January. Inside the front cover of the thicker volume, lettered *eighteen seventy-two to eighteen seventy-nine*, the white edge of Tyler’s own witnessed January protest still showed crisp and square.

Tyler swung the heavy cover open. Straight to the back third of the book he turned. His blunt forefinger stopped under the neat brown heading at folio one hundred and fourteen.

“Three items standing since seventy-seven,” Tyler read out in the exact dry voice he used for widow-meal and bar iron. “December nineteenth, cash advanced Doctor Garland for night ride up Elk Cove, six dollars even. April eighth, one chilled cast-iron plow point, one dollar forty. August tenth, unpaid balance on half-share of one three-year-old bay mule, twelve dollars. Footing, nineteen dollars and forty cents.”

Nineteen dollars and forty cents stood on the leaf. Up Cranberry Creek a grown man swung an axe more than twenty-five ten-hour days from dawn to dark to clear that sum.

Parrish drew a tied buckskin pouch from inside his riding cape. He did not look at Matthew. Not one coin came within an arm’s reach of the boy’s fingers. Stepping to the oak counter directly before Hoss Tyler’s inkwell, Parrish untied the drawstring and counted bright United States silver out across the oiled board in four neat stacks: thirty-six silver half-dollars, four quarters, and four thin dimes.

Matthew stood motionless beside the salt barrels. Once his eyes touched the bright silver rows. Then they dropped to the fine stitched calfskin vamps of Parrish’s riding boots and stayed fixed on the pine floor.

Hoss Tyler picked up two silver half-dollars between thumb and finger. One after another he dropped them onto the hard walnut slab. Both pieces rang clear and true as a chapel bell. Tyler pulled open his wooden till drawer beneath the desk, swept all forty-four silver coins cleanly inside with his palm, and dipped his steel pen deep into black gall ink.

Bending over folio one hundred and fourteen, Tyler spoke every word aloud as his upright nib scratched firm across the ruled page.

“Settled in full, nineteen dollars and forty cents, fourth of March, eighteen seventy-nine — by order of the agent. Hosea Tyler.”

Tyler lifted his perforated tin shaker. Dry blotting-sand whispered across the wet brown ink. He set his left thumb on the calfskin edge to shut the brass clasp.

Before the cover rose an inch, the Deputy United States Marshal stepped one pace from the front door. He laid a stiff, thrice-folded foolscap sheet beside the stoneware inkwell.

“Subpoena and seizure receipt out of the United States Commissioner’s office at Bakersville, Mister Tyler,” the Deputy Marshal said flatly. “Impounding both original day books of Hosea Tyler — eighteen sixty-one to eighteen seventy-one, and eighteen seventy-two to eighteen seventy-nine — for production before the Circuit Court of the United States at Statesville on the third Monday of April next.”

Three full heartbeats passed. Hoss Tyler’s big veined hands stayed locked on the edges of his open ledger while nineteen dollars and forty cents of government silver sat warm inside his till drawer underneath. His gray jaw set hard as creek flint. Down on the open page shone his own wet receipt — *by order of the agent* — bound inside the same book his January protest had certified true for federal court.

Slowly Tyler took his hands off the leather. Without another word he closed the heavy cover over folio one hundred and fourteen.

The Deputy Marshal unrolled a square of black tarred oilcloth on the pine counter. Neat inside went both calfskin volumes. Buckling the leather straps tight, he stepped out onto the front porch to lash the parcel behind his saddle and pulled the door nearly shut behind him.

Inside the quiet store Parrish unfastened the top frog of his wet cape. He clicked open his silver pocket watch under the brass lamp. Ten minutes past six.

“You asked that the warrant run this morning,” Parrish said quietly, looking straight at Matthew now.

Matthew did not glance toward the till drawer. Both bare thumbs worked fast along the damp rim of his wool cap. Speaking once, low and rapid toward the oak counter, the boy gave the day he had named.

“This morning — Tuesday, the fourth,” Matthew said, breath catching thin in his throat. “Father raked the last live fire out from under the kettle inside Mother’s lower barn Saturday night at midnight. Every full jug went off the forty acres before Sunday sunup. Copper cap’s sitting unbolted on the hay-mow floor over stone-cold ashes. Caleb rode up Beech Creek an hour before light after strayed heifers. Nobody’s at the house right now save Mother and Father at the kitchen table.”

Six feet behind the plank wall in the dark scale-room, Joshua’s fingers locked silent onto the rough pine edge of the flour bin. Sharp splinters bit into his palm.

Parrish snapped his silver watch shut and slid it into his waistcoat pocket. “A cold furnace and an empty yard,” Parrish said evenly. “Then no man at your father’s house has cause to lift a hand.”

Matthew took half a step closer to the counter. Both hands twisted the damp wool cap hard against his coat buttons.

“And the paper with my name on it, Mister Parrish,” Matthew said. “A clerk’s line down to Collector Mott’s office at Statesville, or a deputy’s start over in Johnson City, so I’ve got a place to show when the noon mail hack crosses State Line Gap.”

Parrish drew his bare right glove smoothly back over his knuckles. Cleanly he hooked the brass throat-chain of his cape into its link. His face held neither contempt nor hurry, only grave, level courtesy.

“That is a difficulty of the service, Matthew,” Parrish said gently. “Under Commissioner Raum’s rules since August of seventy-six, no Internal Revenue Agent holds authority to commission deputies or place any written promise of office in a witness’s hand before court. A letter from me today would taint my return at Washington and ruin your standing on the April docket.”

Matthew’s thumbs stopped dead on his cap brim. All color drained out of his young face under the yellow lamp.

“When I rode down to Bakersville last month—” Matthew began, voice cracking dry.

“I heard what you asked, and two parts of it stand kept to the letter,” Parrish answered in that same unhurried, courteous tone. “After the Statesville sitting closes in April, I shall forward a voucher to Washington recommending such discretionary allowance as the Commissioner and the Secretary approve. Leave a post-office direction with the postmaster at Johnson City when you cross the state line, so the Treasury order reaches you.”

Matthew stood rigid beside the salt barrels. Not a silver dime sat in his pocket. Not a scrap of paper carried his name. Behind Tyler’s corn-crib stood a bay mule he could never ride back up Elk Cove while he lived.

---

Parrish touched two gloved fingers to his hat brim toward the walnut desk. “Good day, Mister Tyler.”

Pulling the front door wide into the rolling white fog, Parrish stepped out onto the top porch plank. Past Tyler’s shoulder through the dusty front sash, Parrish paused and raised two gloved fingers toward the north mouth of the turnpike.

Two hundred yards up the road, squarely across the mouth of Elk Cove below William Harper’s meadow, sat five mounted men and a laden government pack-horse, dim as iron statues in the mist. Parrish had placed them there before six o’clock so no horseman from Cranberry Forks could ride past them or reach Beech Knob. At Parrish’s lifted hand, all five reined northward into the hollow road while Parrish and the Deputy Marshal swung into their saddles behind them.

Inside the front storehouse Matthew had not stirred one inch from the salt barrels. Head bent low over his crushed wool cap, the boy stared straight down at the bare oak spot beside the inkwell where nineteen dollars and forty cents had lain three minutes ago.

Behind the walnut desk Hoss Tyler turned slowly toward the dim counting-house doorway. Across six feet of shadow the old storekeeper’s flat gray eyes met Joshua’s.

Neither man spoke a word. Tyler did not bar the rear door and offered no excuse. His big hand rested square on the brass knob of his shut till drawer.

Joshua stepped backward without a sound on the flour-dusted boards. He eased the rear crib door open six inches and slipped out into the blind white fog.

Behind the log corn-crib Matthew’s bay mule turned its head over the cedar rail as Joshua ran past. Reaching his claybank mare, Joshua snatched her halter-strap loose, drew his clasp-knife with his teeth, and slashed twice upward through the rawhide pack-lashings. Both hundred-and-twelve-pound bags of bolted flour thudded heavy into the red mud beside Tyler’s sill.

Seven armed horsemen already held the lower creek road at the mouth of Elk Cove. Joshua swung himself belly-down across the bare wooden pack-tree without stirrups and wrenched the mare’s head hard left off the gravel. Driving both boot-heels into her ribs, he sent her straight up the steep rocky sheep-trace through dripping rhododendron laurel and black hemlock behind Beech Knob.

Iron calks sparked and slid on wet ironstone shelves. Low laurel boughs whipped freezing water across Joshua’s beard and eyes. Up the steep pitch the mare lunged in short, heaving bounds. White lather broke hot along her neck-strap while Joshua leaned flat over her withers to save her wind.

Half a mile in, high on the rocky saddle where the sheep-trace curved west above the back timber of William Harper’s junction farm, the mare scrambled onto flat moss. Two hundred feet below Joshua’s left boot, buried invisible under dense white creek-fog, lay the rear cow-lane and split-rail fence behind Harper’s double-crib barn.

Down in that blind fog loose creek-stones clattered fast. Light boots ran hard through wet laurel. No turkey-bone call sounded on the ridge.

A man’s rough voice barked out of the mist below. “Halt there! Drop that jug!”

Stoneware smashed sharp against rock. Right behind the crash, one flat metallic carbine crack punched straight up through the dripping hemlocks.

Wet crows burst silent out of the bare white oaks along Beech Knob. Down behind Harper’s barn a second horseman shouted once, hoarse and short. Then the fog closed dead still over the junction save for six sets of iron shoes splashing steadily on up Elk Cove toward Margaret’s barn.

Joshua drove both heels hard into the lathered mare’s flanks and plunged down the steep laurel wall toward his brother’s yard.
