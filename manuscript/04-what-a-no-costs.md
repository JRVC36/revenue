## Chapter 4: What a No Costs

Late October wind stripped the last yellow hickory leaves off the bank. Bright scraps spun quick across the headrace.

Down in the drained wheel pit Joshua braced one boot against the wet stone wall. He drew the iron wrench a quarter-turn tighter on the last nut. Under his knuckles the forged strap of three-quarter-inch Cranberry flat iron bit snug into the split white-oak rim of the waterwheel. Leak water from the shut flume board dripped icy down his collar.

He stepped back on the slick plank. With his boot heel he knocked the oak scotch out.

Slowly the twelve-foot wheel crept half a bucket downward toward the iron patch, pulled heavy by the bolted iron strap, then stopped still. Joshua reached into his apron pocket, drew out a strip of hammered lead saved from old tea-chest lining, and tacked it beneath the opposite bucket rim with three short clout nails. He tested the rim with two fingers. The big wheel hung dead level on its center. Not an ounce favored either side. He took the cow horn of warm mutton tallow from the wall chink and greased the iron axle neck.

Alone beneath the dripping buckets, he counted days against meal.

Today was Saturday, the twenty-sixth of October. Fifteen days stood left until the tenth of November. Because the tenth fell on a Sunday this year, Hoss Tyler’s twenty-four dollars had to lie on the walnut counter by Saturday sundown on the ninth or Monday noon at the latest. Up in the cabin drawer the oilskin pouch lacked nothing now. Thursday’s wagon haul of six bushels of fine-bolted bread meal to the bloomery boardinghouse had brought in the last two dollars and forty cents. Even so, laying down every hard piece in the house left his till bare as a swept stone before the December freeze locked the pond. One cracked axle bearing or a split hoop between frost and March would send him down to Tyler’s safe hat in hand.

Up on the rocky bench track a single shod hoof struck flint.

Joshua’s thumb stopped on the greased iron. Mountain mules came up Elk Cove short-stepping and slack-shod. Four light shoes clicked uneven over loose slate. Whatever walked down toward the boundary oak moved long-strided and flat-shod. Three full breaths passed before the next forefoot came down.

Joshua wiped tallow off his palm onto his thigh. Iron wrench in his left hand, he climbed the six oak rungs.

He crossed the dim mill floor without waking a board. Sunlight struck him on the sill.

Halfway down the bench grade, abreast of the scarred boundary oak and the granite cornerstone, sat William Parrish on his bay gelding.

Nobody rode behind him. No deputy waited in the laurel toward the turnpike. Parrish wore a heavy dark riding coat buttoned to his chin against the ridge wind, gray wool trousers strapped snug under his boot arches, and brown leather gloves creased at the knuckles. All four of the gelding’s iron shoes stood planted two inches uphill of the granite post on Joshua’s side of the line.

Parrish did not look up toward the millpond. Both gloved hands rested quiet on the pommel while he sighted straight down the unfenced cart ruts and the split-chestnut water trough carrying Joshua’s tailrace across the unmarked grass to Margaret’s lower log barn forty paces below her kitchen porch.

Everything down on Margaret’s forty acres lay plain in the thin noon sun. Fresh-split chestnut and hickory billets stood corded chest-high along the lower barn eaves, four times what any kitchen hearth burned before Christmas. Two scalded oak mash tubs leaned bottom-up on poles beside the sill. Pale woodsmoke rose thin from the dry-laid stone flue behind the roof.

Down on the house porch Margaret stepped out carrying an empty cedar water pail. At the top step she halted with one hand on the locust post. Straight up the slope she looked at the horseman by her father’s stone. Neither calling toward the barn nor hurrying back indoors, she walked steady across the swept yard to the spring trough by the smokehouse. She dipped her pail full, lifted it clear without spilling a drop on her apron hem, carried it back inside the kitchen, and latched the batten door behind her.

Up by the forked oak Parrish lifted his eyes to the mill sill.

He touched two gloved fingers to his hat brim. Without setting one hoof past the granite post onto Margaret’s turf, he reined the bay gelding neatly round in its own length. He rode slow up the rocky grade. At Joshua’s hitch rail he drew halt.

Swinging down easily, he looped the leather rein once round the worn locust bar and walked across the chips to the mill door like any farmer come out of the wind.

---

Dry corn dust hung faint in the cold shaft through the south shutter. Outside the logs the bypass water hissed down the slate ditch.

Parrish stepped over the worn oak threshold and halted beside the meal chest. He took off neither his hat nor his brown riding gloves. For a moment he studied the level hang of the upper French millstone suspended two inches clear of the lower stone on its iron bail. His eyes moved once to the bright brass beam of the hanging scale on its peg and the county-sealed half-bushel measure sitting mouth-down on clean pine. He did not glance toward the open shelf where Joshua’s chalk board hung.

“Good morning to you, Mister Brennan,” Parrish said.

“Morning,” Joshua said. He set the tallow horn on the window ledge. The square iron wrench stayed in his hand.

“That stone hangs true,” Parrish observed, nodding toward the dressed face. “Half the country mills between Marion and Boone let the iron shaft wear slack until one side grinds flour and the other turns out whole grits. Your father set this frame?”

“Forty-eight,” Joshua said. “I put in the French stone after sixty-five.”

“Sound white oak in the timbers,” Parrish said. He turned half round from the stone without resting elbow or hip against Joshua’s clean meal chest, looking straight across the four feet of swept plank between them. “You told me the road stayed passable to your dam. You keep an exact tongue, Mister Brennan.”

Joshua waited.

“When a revenue officer comes into a strange county,” Parrish said in the same quiet, level voice he had used above the saddler’s shop, “county politicians offer him twenty names before he pulls his boots off. Half are personal grudges left over from the war. Landless boys make up the rest, boiling ten gallons in borrowed copper. Chasing boys through laurel thickets wastes government mileage and changes nothing by June.”

Wind rattled a loose shingle over the garner loft. A dry yellow leaf blew across the sill and settled beside Parrish’s blacked boot toe.

“Down in *Deed Book D* at Bakersville,” Parrish continued, “your mill tract stands entered at eighty-six acres in fee, subject to a deed of trust for four hundred dollars held by Mister Hosea Tyler at the turnpike fork. Twenty-four dollars annual interest falls due each tenth of November. Directly below your white-oak corner lie forty acres conveyed ten summers back to Margaret Brennan as her sole and separate estate out of her father’s old grant. You own no share in that lower forty, and you keep no copper on your eighty-six.”

“No,” Joshua said.

“That is why I rode up here alone at noon instead of coming with deputy marshals before day,” Parrish said. “Washington repealed moieties in seventy-two, as I told your neighbors. What the commissioner does authorize under the detective appropriation is regular per-diem subsistence and information vouchers paid in United States notes upon my certificate alone. No voucher prints a local name on any court roll at Statesville. No witness summons ever issues.”

Parrish drew neither pocketbook nor greenback into the light. Both gloved hands stayed quiet at his coat buttons.

“Three lines sent down to Bakersville once a fortnight,” Parrish said, “setting down whose mules bring eight bushels of chop at a trip, which nights the copper caps go on along this creek, and what wagons take the full kegs below the Forge, pay twenty dollars the month from November through March. That comes to one hundred dollars before April court sits. Enough to pay your November interest four times over and cut Mister Tyler’s principal down.”

Outside in the ditch cold water slid unbroken over slate.

“I grind corn,” Joshua said.

Parrish did not blink or shift weight. “Every public miller in North Carolina grinds corn, Mister Brennan. Few millers send their tailrace down a split-chestnut trough straight beside the mash tubs of an unbonded eighty-gallon furnace forty paces below a woman’s kitchen.”

Joshua’s fingers stayed still on the cold iron wrench.

“You think silence sits in the middle between your brother and the law,” Parrish said without heat. “Before sixty-eight, silence did sit in the middle. Under the present statute it does not. This January the Supreme Court ruled flatly upon section forty-four. A land forfeiture under that section does not try the owner’s temper. It tries the ground used in the business.”

He turned his head two inches toward the open mill door. Beyond the sill the rutted track dropped past the white oak.

“Where untaxed spirits are run in an unbonded barn on one deed,” Parrish said, “and the meal comes coarse off the adjoining stones, the condensing water runs out of the adjoining race, and one unfenced cart road carries wood and kegs across the line without a gate, the libel joins the whole connected premise.”

Parrish lifted one gloved hand an inch and let it settle back against his coat skirt.

“More than that,” he said. “Under sections thirty-two eighty-one and fifty-four forty, any proprietor who knowingly suffers or permits his mill water or his road to serve an illicit distillery answers by indictment alongside the man firing the furnace. Six months to two years in prison, a thousand-dollar fine, and the United States forfeiture lien cutting clean ahead of your creditor. A man with an encumbrance at Bakersville cannot afford a federal lien ahead of his creditor, Mister Brennan.”

“My dam waters my wheel,” Joshua said. “What runs past my wheel goes down the natural hollow where water ran before North Carolina was surveyed.”

“Water runs down rock by nature,” Parrish said. “Water does not hew a chestnut trough and prop it on forked locust stakes across a boundary stone by nature.”

Five slow breaths went past between them on the dim floor. Neither raised his chin or dropped his gaze.

“Come up my stair in Bakersville on any Tuesday before hard freeze, Mister Brennan,” Parrish said, “and file a plain sworn statement setting out where your mill use ends at that granite post. Do that before a warrant issues, and the line holds at that oak. Wait until deputy marshals ride down your cart track behind a gauger, and I must certify the whole connected premise from the dam apron to the lower barn.”

“No,” Joshua said.

Parrish studied the spare gray beard and steady mouth before him. Once he inclined his head.

Joshua laid the iron wrench on the oak timber beside the hopper. At the garner wall he took a two-bushel sack by the ears, swung its hundredweight onto the hopper frame without grunting, and untied the hickory string at its neck.

“The road’s open down the creek, Mister Parrish,” Joshua said.

“Good day to you, Mister Brennan,” Parrish answered.

Stepping out across the sunny sill, he untied the bay gelding and swung into leather. Without pausing again at the white-oak stone or turning his head toward Margaret’s barn, he walked the bay down the hollow trace until the dark laurel at the bend hid horse and coat together.

---

Joshua did not let water onto the wheel.

Inside the doorway he waited until the last iron shoe click died out. Then he drew the heavy mill shutter to, dropped the oak latch bar into its staple, and climbed the steep slate path behind the pond to his cabin.

Wind off Big Yellow hummed dry through dead jimson stalks by the step. Inside, he shut the split-oak door and slid the wooden bolt home.

Gray ash lay cold over last night’s hickory coals on the hearth. Beside the andirons sat a three-legged iron skillet scraped bright with sand and one tin cup turned bottom-up on the clean hearthstone. Across the room the batten door to the inner chamber stood latched shut on its wooden pin as it stayed from frost to frost, leaving only his narrow cord bed in the corner and one washed hickory shirt hanging from a locust peg. Only the small shelf clock ticked high on the mantelboard.

Alone in the low room, Joshua stood by the hearth post. Scrubbed poplar boards lay bare under his boots. Reaching behind the clock case, he lifted a small brass key from its nail.

Under the south window stood the small walnut table his father had brought over the Blue Ridge in a covered wagon forty years back. Joshua fitted the brass bit into the keyhole, turned it until the spring bolt snapped back, and drew the deep drawer into the light.

Right in front lay the tied oilskin pouch.

He loosened the leather string and counted every piece out onto the bare walnut top in separate piles so no coin touched its neighbor. Thirty-six worn silver half-dollars stood stacked six high for eighteen dollars. Four silver quarters made nineteen. One creased five-dollar United States note brought the count to twenty-four dollars even. Every piece stood for white bread meal struck level in a half-bushel tub four pounds at a grinding since dog days, hauled down rocky ruts behind the claybank mare and sold out at forty cents and fifty cents while town buyers pinched the flour between their thumbs.

Three-line schedules sent to Bakersville bought that pile four times over in one winter.

Joshua looked into the open drawer behind the front strip. No dime had slipped into the crack. With two fingers he lifted the gray homespun flannel lining the bottom.

Three papers lay side by side on the bare poplar board.

On top lay Hoss Tyler’s narrow white receipt from the tenth of November last autumn. Upright black ink crossed the slip: *Received of Joshua Brennan twenty-four dollars lawful money, one year’s interest to date upon four hundred dollars secured by deed of trust on the Elk Cove mill tract.*

Half showing beneath the receipt lay a stitched double leaf of coarse blue-lined foolscap. Across its head stood the years *eighteen sixty-six*, *eighteen sixty-seven*, *eighteen sixty-eight*, and *eighteen sixty-nine* written in Joshua’s own square hand in faded brown walnut-hull ink, five years before his mill paper ever went into Hoss Tyler’s iron safe. No boy’s Christian name stood anywhere on the sheet. Down the left margin ran only the month and *To Jacob’s house*:

*December: four yards stout gray jeans, two dollars ten.*
*January: one pair pegged kip shoes, small, one dollar sixty.*
*September: slate, stone pencil, and primer, forty cents.*
*November: cash in silver for winter keeping, five dollars.*

Under the stitched foolscap leaf, rubbed soft as dressed buckskin along its three folds, lay a stiff oblong of heavy government rag paper headed by a spread-winged eagle dimmed brown with age. It had been signed at Knoxville, Tennessee, in August eighteen sixty-five: a United States Army discharge. Folded inward so neither troop letter nor regiment showed upward in the light, the paper rested with its lower edge even against last November’s mortgage receipt.

For half a minute no sound moved in the shut room save a dry oak leaf sliding across the roof shakes overhead and the small brass pendulum swinging behind the glass door on the mantel.

Lifting none of them from its place, Joshua laid the gray flannel smooth over all three papers. He swept the thirty-six half-dollars, the four quarters, and the five-dollar greenback back into the oilskin pouch, knotted the leather string tight, and set the pouch on the flannel before locking the walnut drawer.

After hanging the key back behind the clock, he unbolted his door and went down.

---

Cloud shadow had slid over the cove by three o’clock. Cold pewter dulled the millpond.

Joshua walked past the barred millhouse without stopping. Down the rutted bench track yellow hickory leaves had already drifted into the four round shoe prints where Parrish’s bay gelding had stood beside the forked white oak and the granite cornerstone. Not one print crossed the stone.

Joshua halted two paces uphill of the granite post on his own turf.

Saturday work went brisk in the raw wind round Margaret’s lower barn. Caleb had thrown off his wool roundabout and stood in rolled shirtsleeves at the chopping block, swinging a five-pound poll axe into seasoned chestnut bolts from the shoulder. His broad wrists dropped on the helve without hurry, splitting every bolt through the heart at a single blow.

Twenty feet lower, right beside the split-chestnut trough where Joshua’s cold tailrace splashed into the spring branch, Jacob knelt over an eighty-gallon mash barrel laid on its side. He poured a steaming bucket of scalding kettle water through the bunghole, drove a wooden plug snug with his palm, and rolled the heavy oak staves hard back and forth over two peeled skids to sweeten the sour meal crust inside. Beside the skids stood a covered half-bushel of kiln-dried sprouted corn malt and a gourd of winter rye meal to cap the next set of mash. Over by the corncrib corner Matthew sat hunched on an upturned half-bushel tub beside the bay mule, scraping dried red turnpike clay off the welts of his brogans with an old case knife.

Jacob straightened from the barrel and wiped wet ash off his wrist. “Fetch me that long white-oak scraper off the sill, Son, before this water chills.”

Caleb drove his axe blade deep into the chestnut block. Taking the wooden scraper off the sill, he laid it in Jacob’s palm.

Up by the house porch Margaret’s kitchen shutter stayed closed, though gray woodsmoke blew low from the stone chimney toward the creek.

Joshua stepped off the bench track, crossed the wet footlog over the branch where the chestnut trough joined the spring run, and walked onto Margaret’s forty acres.

Matthew’s case knife stopped first on his boot welt. Caleb turned half round from the woodpile, one hand light on the axe helve.

“Jacob,” Joshua said across the steaming barrel.

Jacob looked up through the white vapor rising into the wind. Once his eyes moved from Joshua’s empty hands to his buttoned coat. He stood straight.

“Wash your hands when that tub’s drained,” Joshua said. “That man from Bakersville sat his horse at our corner oak at noon.”
