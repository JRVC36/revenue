# Pitch & Blurb — *REVENUE*

**Status: v1 — the pitch is written to the Style Guide's §1 test (genre promise stated in the pitch
itself, a named protagonist with a want, other characters with their own intentions), and it is honest
about the fact that the book is not yet drafted. Do not send this anywhere until the manuscript exists;
send it to the author now.**

---

## 1. The one line

**In the mountains of North Carolina, 1878, the only acreage in the hollow that no creditor can reach
belongs to a man's wife — and that is exactly where the still stands.**

## 2. The paragraph (back-cover length)

> The tax is ninety cents on the proof gallon. A bushel of corn makes two and a half gallons and costs
> a quarter. That is the whole book in arithmetic.
>
> Jacob Brennan is the younger brother and owns no land, so the still sits on the only ground in the cove his
> debts cannot touch: forty acres his wife holds in her own name, inherited from her father, beyond the reach
> of any merchant under the state constitution of 1868. It is also why he cannot register. A bond required an
> owner in fee free of every lien, or the written consent of every lienholder, and the only unencumbered
> dirt in the hollow belonged to a married woman who never asked for the privilege.
>
> When a revenue agent arrives with a list and a ledger, the cove does what it has always done: it does not
> know. The trick does not work on Margaret Brennan, because the ground the still stands on is hers, and an
> owner forfeits her land whether she knew or not. Nor does it work on Joshua, the elder brother who
> inherited the farm next door and mortgaged it — the government intends to reach his acreage too, by
> arguing that two places watered by one stream are one place. Joshua came home from the war a farmer and
> went into the militia an officer; he keeps the peace by keeping the books, and pays off the mortgage in
> installments he never explains. Matthew, nineteen, wants out, and the government's own informer law
> quietly stopped paying informers six years before he was asked, which he will learn after he has learned
> everything.
>
> By the time a barn burns, the question is not who owed the tax. It is what Margaret knew, what Joshua
> permitted, and whether a woman with a deed her husband cannot sign can make the United States answer to
> her in a courtroom two counties away.

## 3. Genre promise (Guide §1: "quiet literary realism" is not a promise)

**Literary historical fiction with a prosecution at its center.** The promise is: an accurate material
life, a legal mechanism that turns out to be the plot, and a court scene where the horror is paperwork.
Not a feud saga; not a moonshine adventure; nobody wins a shoot-out.

- **Length:** 85,000–100,000 words, 4 acts (the outline's 3 acts split at the barn fire), **28 chapters**
  (8 / 9 / 11) plus three printed documents at the end, 4–7 scenes each — `canon/canon-v1.md` §12.
- **Timeline:** summer 1878 → **November 1881**, on the real court calendar: Jacob is convicted at the term
  that opened 21 April 1879 and released on the two years on 21 April 1881; his nephew is tried at the term
  that opened 17 October 1881, and the book's last page is seven months before the rails reach Elk Park. The railroad is four acts of approach and one withheld
  picture: powder heard off a ridge at the opening, powder heard off a ridge at the close, and **no train
  on the page at any date** — in June 1882 the rails reached Elk Park, thirty miles and a year past the
  last sentence of this book.
- **POV:** third person limited, past tense. **Joshua Brennan carries ~80%; Margaret Brennan ~20%** (canon R-4). Nobody else is ever inside a head — which is why a book about an informer reads like an interrogation of the reader.

## 4. Shelling out

Two real neighbors, both to be confirmed with the author before any query:

| Use | Why | How to say it |
|---|---|---|
| *Moonshine: Its History and Folklore* (Horace Kephart) | the book's factual bedrock, and the reason readers trust the still-talk | "for readers of Kephart who wish it were a novel" |
| *Cold Mountain* (Charles Frazier) | the mountain-voice-without-eye-dialect precedent, and the bar to clear | "without the romance plot or the picaresque" |
| *The Whiskey Rebels* (David Liss) | the 1790s book — cite it only to distinguish | "**not** the Whiskey Rebellion; this is the one nobody remembers, because the government won it" |

Do **not** pitch it against *Hillbillies*-as-satire anything, and do not let a query imply the mountaineers
are charming. They are right about the arithmetic.

## 5. Blurb (for the title page, if the author ever wants one)

> **Ninety cents on the proof gallon.**
>
> In 1878, in a cove of western North Carolina with a name nobody outside it can spell, the whiskey tax
> is not a grievance. It is weather. Then a young agent from the Treasury arrives with a list, and the
> old law that everyone in the hollow has been hiding behind turns out to be the one that takes the farm:
> the land itself pays for the still, whether the owner consents or not.
>
> *REVENUE* is a novel about a family that survives by not knowing, and the year that not knowing
> stopped being a defense.

## 6. The sentence that must survive into the finished book

From the outline, Act Four as currently drafted, with the mechanism corrected per R-3:

> "You didn't sell a man. You answered a notice."

If the whole book can carry that line without embarrassment, it is finished. If it can't, the trial
chapter is wrong, not the line.
