## 1. Three things I want to say before the question list

**1.1 The outline doesn't know what format it is.** It is titled "A Three-Act Drama" and speaks in
screen/stage idiom throughout: "Opening image," "Final image of Act One," "the play can go a few
directions here," "In a voiceover," "Titles or voiceover." But your Style Guide §6.1–6.3, your
*Bound Tree* precedent, and the whole revision apparatus (close third, the 80/20 split, the
interiority rule, chapter-ending momentum passes, Kokoro audiobook build) are **novel** machinery.
This isn't pedantry: the interiority rule (§2.3) and the "engine of discovery" (§2.1) are the two
things this outline is furthest from satisfying, and both only exist in prose. Decide this first;
everything downstream depends on it.

**1.2 The railroad is already in your story and you left it out.** Your `Rough Research` doc says the
ET&WNC didn't reach Cranberry until 1882, and recommends moving the book to the 1880s–90s to get
that. **Don't move the book. Move the train into it.** Construction on the narrow gauge began
September 1880; the line hit Elk Park mid-June 1882 and Cranberry on July 3, 1882 (Avery Journal
county history; ET&WNC Historical Society). So through Act Two and Act Three — exactly the years of
your siege — a railroad is being blasted into the mountains twenty miles below the cove with
black powder, Northern money, immigrant labor, and cash wages. Your characters would hear it. It
also gives the ending its real argument: the tax didn't kill Jacob's world, and neither did Caleb's
rifle. The connection did, and it arrives eighteen months after the last scene. That is a free,
verifiable, devastating final image — and it's the same surveyor-versus-the-mountains machinery you
already built in *The Bound Tree*.

**1.3 Your best thematic line is a legal fact you haven't used yet.** Joshua tells Jacob: *"Right
doesn't matter. They have guns and paper."* In this period that is nearly technical. The operative
statute (Rev. Stat. §§ 3258, 3281, as re-enacted by Act of Feb. 8, 1875, § 16) forfeits not only the
still and the whiskey but **the lot or tract of land on which the distillery stands**, and further
forfeits the interest of *"every person who knowingly has suffered or permitted the business of a
distiller to be there carried on, or has connived at the same."* (*United States v. Stowell*, 133
U.S. 1; *Dobbins' Distillery v. United States*, 96 U.S. 395 — the latter holds a **landlord**
forfeits even when the tenant's fraud was unknown to him.) Consequences for your book:

- Parrish doesn't need to prove Jacob owes 90¢ a gallon. He needs to prove **Harper knew**. The
  barn fire scene becomes a scene about *knowledge and consent* — the whole moral weight of Act Two
  lands on whether a sixty-year-old man let a jug sit in his barn.
- **Joshua is legally finished from page one.** He is a miller and trader who owns land. The moment
  Parrish names him in an information, Joshua's fee is under the government's thumb even if Joshua
  never touches a barrel. His "I won't inform" is not a stance. It is a mortgage payment.
- Jacob's hollow itself — held, I'd guess, by unrecorded possession — is the thing at stake. Not the
  copper. The mountain.

That engine is period-exact, verifiable, physical, and it makes the title literal. Build the plot on
it (Guide §1.2, "Never hang the stakes of a novel on an abstract or invented technicality").

## 2. Research I need from you

### R1. Which county are we actually in?
The outline sets the story near **Banner Elk / Elk Park**, which in 1878 was **Watauga County** (seat:
Boone), formed 1849 from Ashe, Caldwell, Wilkes, Yancey. **Avery County does not exist until 1911.**
Your `Rough Research` doc instead runs the jurisdiction history of *Cranberry* (1840–61 Burke;
1861–1911 **Mitchell**) and then says "Banner Elk, Elk Park" as if they were the same record set. They
are adjacent but different counties with different courthouses. Since the whole plot turns on records
— deeds, liens, court terms, the register's book — the county is not scenery.
> **I need back:** a decision on the exact township/hollow, plus which county's records we're using:
> Watauga (Boone), Mitchell (Burnsville), or Caldwell (Burkeville)? Do the Brennans hold by recorded
> deed, unrecorded deed, or parol possession? Any 1870s NC tax-delinquency **tax sales** or "Act to
> quiet titles" wrinkle in that county?

### R2. The court, the term calendar, and the actual numbers
Jacob's trial is at "the federal courthouse in Asheville or Greeneville, Tennessee." The Western
District of NC was created 4 June 1872 and its first judge was **Robert Paine Dick** (1823–1898), a
Grant appointee, ex-Confederate legislator, former state supreme court justice — NCPedia notes he was
"recognized for his **kindly temper** in administrating the internal revenue laws." That is a real
judge for your sentencing scene and he is *not* a villain, which is exactly your Guide §1.3.
I could not confirm from here that Asheville had a sitting U.S. district term in 1879 (Asheville's
first purpose-built federal courthouse dates to **1892**; one NC Reports list gives WDNC district-court
terms at **Statesville** (3rd Monday April & October) and elsewhere). Court *sittings* are load-bearing
for you: they set the clock on Parrish's raids and Matthew's information.
> **I need back:** (a) where the WDNC district and circuit courts actually sat in 1878–81 and on what
> dates; (b) which county Watauga/Caldwell cases were tried in; (c) who the U.S. Attorney (district
> attorney) was 1878–81 and whether local juries convicted; (d) real sentence lengths imposed on
> western NC distillers in 1879–81 — did anyone actually get two years? (e) venue: could a NC
> distiller ever be tried in Greeneville, TN, or does that have to be cut?

### R3. The money, in dollars a mountain farmer could count
The tax during your whole window is **90¢ per proof gallon**, set 3 March 1875 and unmoved until 1894
(TTB historical rate table; a period source writing in 1881 confirms "at which rate it has remained
up to the date of the present writing"). Meanwhile a bushel of corn marketed at ~25¢ yields about
2.5 gallons of whiskey worth ~$1.25. So the tax is roughly **the whole value of the whiskey and more**
— the 1791 grievance, mathematically re-created. I want every figure in the book to survive a reader
with a calculator.
> **I need back:** a small table of period numbers for 1878–81 in that county — bushel of corn, rye,
> wheat; a gallon of whiskey at the still and in the cove; salt, sugar, coffee, tobacco, cloth per
> unit; a hand's wages (day/month); a mule; a cow; land per acre; blacksmith's rates; a jug; a barrel;
> a deputy's pay; a revenue agent's salary; a miller's toll (peck per bushel); **and what Parrish
> could realistically offer Matthew in 1879 dollars** so "good money" means something.

### R4. Informers: who paid, how much, and on whose say-so
This is the pivot of Act One, and there's a real, cruel mechanism available. The **Act of July 20,
1868** barred approving a distiller's bond unless he was *"owner in fee, unencumbered by any
mortgage, judgment, or other lien"* of the land — otherwise he needed the written consent of the fee
owner *and every lienholder*, stipulating the United States's lien came **first**. That single clause
legally excludes almost every mountain farmer from going legal. And on informing: an informer's share
of fines and forfeitures was nominally a moiety (one half), **but from 1 August 1872 moieties were
repealed and replaced by discretionary payments** determined by the Commissioner of Internal Revenue
with the Secretary of the Treasury's approval, capped by regulation. Meaning: by 1879 **the traitor's
paycheck is a favor from Washington, granted or withheld at discretion.** Matthew could inform, burn
his father, and be told no. That is the best beat in your second act and it's free.
> **I need back:** (a) confirmation of the 1872 moiety repeal and what "informer's share" actually
> paid in a rural NC case 1878–81; (b) whether an agent could promise a *reward* without lying; (c)
> how a "moiler" was regarded, and any western NC example of an informer being attacked, tarred,
> whipped, or killed in 1876–1885; (d) whether the government ever paid nothing.

### R5. What a raid actually looked like, start to finish
Your Guide's strength is procedural machinery, and the outline currently has the least procedure in
it. I want the mechanics: how a deputy assembled a posse (who, at what hour, paid how), how a still
was found, what was done with the copper (seized, appraised, advertised, **sold at public auction in
the county seat** — which is why "hauled away" in your Act One is probably wrong), what happened to
the whiskey (also sold, and frequently bought back by the distiller's friends, which your outline's
"poured out" should confront), what a "gauge" was, how proof was tested by beading in a vial, and
what "cutting down the old stands" meant on the ground. A 1879 Montgomery County, TN episode I found
is pure gold and period-right: officers found the still "in full blast" days before Christmas, cut
the still and the stands, and turned the mules loose; the distillers fired on them from the brush at
a hundred yards with squirrel rifles, and a deputy counted about twenty-five "blockaders" holding the
county.
> **I need back:** one fully reconstructed raid (preferably WNC, 1878–82) end to end with sources;
> the disposition of seized property (appraisal, auction notice, sale, who bought); and the vocabulary
> list of the trade as it was actually said in 1878.

### R6. The 1878 amnesty — the question the outline never asks
The Tennessee Encyclopedia records that **in 1878 the federal government offered a blanket pardon to
moonshiners, and many accepted while keeping their stills running.** If anything like that reached
western NC, your entire Act One has a hole in it that Jacob would be asked about at every store: *why
not just take the pardon, register, and be done?* A reader will ask it too. Notably your opening
image has Jacob's still "not hidden in a paranoid way—it's just placed, the way you'd place a
garden" — which is legally impossible, since the 1868 act forbade setting a still in or near a
dwelling or its outbuildings and required registration and a bond. His placement is either a crime
of the statute or a statement of war, and you should pick deliberately.
> **I need back:** did an amnesty/proclamation of 1876–78 operate in NC; who took it; what registering
> a still actually required (bond, sureties, form of still, register number, the "notice of
> intention"), what a legal still cost a small farmer, and how many were registered in Watauga /
> Caldwell / Mitchell / Burke 1875–1885 (the 1880 census manufactures schedule and the distillery
> assessments should give a countable number).

### R7. Prison, if Jacob is in one for two of five years
Sent "to a penitentiary in Kentucky or Virginia" needs an institution. Federal prisoners in 1879 were
housed under contract in state prisons (Louisville's Blocket was 1860s; the Atlanta federal prison is
1899; Lewis Redmond of NC drew ten years at **Auburn, New York** in 1881). Also: your "smuggled out by
a sympathetic guard" needs the rules it breaks, and Caleb's "prison labor" needs a system (NC's
postwar convict lease and the 1870s–80s leasing of convicts to mines, railroads, and turpentine
camps is one of the most brutal and least-read facts of the decade — and it is *revenue*).
> **I need back:** where WDNC federal convicts went 1879–82; a day's regime there; letter rules and
> how often; what a smuggled letter risked; whether NC leased convicts in 1880 and to what work; and
> how a two-year federal sentence in 1879 actually plays out in time (credit, pardon, release
> paperwork).

### R8. Violence, death, and what the Army could do
Your Act Three turns on Joshua's claim that killing a federal agent brings the Army. Between 1876 and
1880 about **25 revenue agents were killed** in the mountain South, ~6/year — real, and small: the
same source notes deputy marshals in the trans-Atlantic West died at ~20/year, and that the national
press blew mountain killings out of proportion. There is also a 150-page study of exactly your
question: **"The Army and Moonshiners in the Mountainous South"** (Fort Leavenworth thesis, ADA284658,
on DTIC) — it holds from 1869 to 1877 the Army was *more* active against moonshiners than in earlier
Reconstruction years. And the U.S. Marshals' own casualty roll for the WDNC in your period: a Deputy
Marshal **Burns, shot in the back from ambush, 13 April 1874, Western District of North Carolina**.
> **I need back:** whether troops were called, asked for, or refused in NC's mountain counties
> 1874–1885 and by whom (governor? agent? Washington?); what a posse actually was vs. soldiers; and
> the legal trigger for intervention (the Enforcement Acts, insurrection, suspension of habeas).
> Also, the 1879 Supreme Court decision *Tennessee v. Davis* (100 U.S. 257) is a moonshine **removal**
> case — a state officer enforcing a state seizure got the prosecution pulled into federal court — and
> your Act Two would be far stranger and better if some lawyer in town knows that.

### R9. Reconstruction, which your outline has deleted
There is no Black character, no church politics, no party name, and no war memory in this book, and it
is set in 1878 North Carolina. Your `Rough Research` doc hand-supplies the alibi (near-absent
slaveholding in the high coves, Unionist mountains, Burke County voting against secession) — and I
believe all of it. But two years after the end of Reconstruction, in these counties, "revenue" was not
a neutral word: the state's postwar **tax sales** of delinquent (Confederate) farms, the 1868
constitution and its schools, the Klan's 1868–72 campaign aimed partly at federal revenue men and
Black officeholders (Rutherford and McDowell counties were the flashpoints), and the railroad-debt
"readjustment" fight were all revenue fights in which mountain farmers were main characters. The
Kirk–Holden war, North Carolina's actual 1870 use of state militia against masked men, is the precedent
your Joshua would cite.
> **I need back — this is as much a direction decision as research:** do you want the cove to be
> politically naive (they have never heard of Reconstruction, which is not believable) or politically
> shaped by it (they lived through it and it is why they distrust every man with paper)? If the latter,
> what did this specific hollow do in 1861–65 and 1868–72, and were there any Black Brennan-adjacent
> families, tenants, or mill workers in it?

### R11. The epilogue is wrong, and its errors matter
- *"Lewis Redmond, a real distiller from Watauga County… He was captured in 1881."* Wrong county and
  wrong shape. Redmond was born 1854/55 and hauled from **Transylvania** County; he shot Deputy U.S.
  Marshal **Alfred Duckworth** on 1 March 1876 (a point-blank derringer in the throat after asking the
  marshal to holster his pistol), fled to the Pickens district of South Carolina, returned to Swain
  County in 1879, was captured **7 April 1881** near present-day Bryson City after being hit by buckshot,
  and was tried **25 August 1881** — **not** for murder, but on eight counts of making, transporting and
  selling untaxed liquor and two conspiracy counts. Ten years at **Auburn, N.Y.**, $2,600 fine, pardoned
  1884 by Arthur, died Walhalla, S.C., 1906. He had a $1,000 reward on his head, a *New York Times*
  piece ("Democratic Moonshiners," 15 July 1878), a national interview from a Charleston reporter who
  found him in June 1878, a **dime novel in 1879** and a rival pamphlet in 1881. He is your perfect
  foil for Caleb — a man made into fiction while the facts were still warm — and the fact that no one
  hanged him for Duckworth tells your readers everything about how these cases were traded.
- *"The 'Whiskey Rebellion' of 1878–1881 in western North Carolina"* — **there is no such event by that
  name.** That title belongs to 1791–94. Contemporaries and the historiography say **"moonshine war"**
  / **"whiskey war"** (Commissioner **Green B. Raum's** 1878 western NC crackdown is the real event your
  Act One is describing, and J.J. Mott and other agents were the ones telling Raum that conditions were
  bleak and that the distillers were armed and backed by whole communities). If the book prints the
  phrase "Whiskey Rebellion of 1878–1881," an informed reader will stop trusting the whole epilogue.
- *"Caleb Brennan's fate is unknown."* — see Q6 below.
> **I need back:** the epilogue rebuilt on sourced facts only, or a decision to cut the pseudo-historical
> frame entirely (my vote, see Q6).

the **stamp** itself (what a tax stamp looks like, who sells it, the collector's cut); telegraph

> **I need back:** a rulings list, one line each, in `docs/period-audit.md` style, before drafting.

### R-13 — What was making noise within hearing of the cove in autumn 1881?

**Why it exists now:** Q-2 was ruled as *the train is heard about, never seen* (canon R-5), which makes the
last sentence of the book a sound — and I refuse to write a sound that could not have been heard. In
October 1881 the ET&WNC's rail head was Hampton, Tennessee (22 Aug 1881); Elk Park is June 1882. Thirty
air miles and two ridges: no engine, no whistle. So the closing noise is either (a) a **grade blast** that
is genuinely in range, (b) a **local** sound Joshua can name and declines to walk to — county road or
bridge work, a quarry, a saw, or (c) **report**: a man who went to work the grade and came home for the
winter with the date.

**What I need back:** Watauga (or the chosen county's) court of sessions minutes 1879–1882 for road
overseers, turnpike contracts and blasting payments; any 1881–82 east-Tennessee paper notice of ET&WNC
work north of Elizabethton; and if the cove moves to Mitchell/Caldwell (Q-12), the same for the Cranberry
works, whose furnaces and iron-mine tramways were locally audible and are *better* than the railroad for
this purpose.

**If nobody can find it:** I default to (c) report + a generic blast, which is safe, and the audit row
stays `NOTE`.

### R-17 — When a mountain family gave a child to a brother, is there any record of it?

**Why it exists now:** Q-5 was ruled as *Caleb is Joshua's firstborn, raised as Jacob's son* (canon R-8).
The arrangement is the book's quiet engine, and it is currently `INVENT` — I would like it to be `INVENT`

and it is checkable in an afternoon; (iv) any 1870s county history, WPA ex-slave/WPA-adjacent oral collection,
or church record that describes the practice in the mountains in the family's own words.

**Why the census point (iii) matters most:** if a Watauga/McDowell family in 1870 shows an 8-year-old boy in
an uncle's household with the father listed two doors down, I can print the arrangement as *typical* and stop
apologizing for it in the author's note. If I cannot find it, the book still works — R-8 stands — but the

**Also wanted while someone is in the same courthouse:** what legal standing a "kept child" had for
inheritance absent a deed or a will — did mountain families write these things down at all?

### R-19 — How an informer's money actually reached a man's hand, 1876–1882

**Why it exists now:** Q-7 was ruled as *three asks, granted / granted / refused*, with the payment arriving
months late as a Treasury order that goes uncashed and is returned marked "person unknown" (canon R-10). The
mechanism is documented at the level of law (moieties repealed for claims accruing after 1 Aug 1872;

mountain novel, so I need the plumbing:

* Was the order payable **at the local depositary bank**, at the agent's office, or only at Washington?
* Did the payer require the claimant's name and a signature, or was it bearer paper? (If it were bearer

  exiled man never collects because collecting means coming back.)
* What does a returned order look like in a file — the clerk's wording, and does the file keep it?
* While there: does any WDNC record show an informer allowance *requested* for the 1878–81 seizures, even if
  refused? One real instance would let me name the amount instead of inventing it.

* In a WDNC internal-revenue matter 1879–82, was a third party's book reached by **subpoena duces tecum**
  from the court, by **seizure under a commissioner's warrant**, or only by producing witness + book
  together? If you can find one docket entry or one newspaper account of a seizure of papers, that is
  enough to draft the scene from.
* **North Carolina law on books of account** in this period — the state's courts were famously restrictive
  about a trader charging himself from his own book; what made a day book admissible against a third party
  (entry in the regular course, no interest in the event, the keeper called to swear to it)?
* What a protest over seized papers looked like: to whom was it addressed (the judge, the marshal, the
  District Attorney), and would a clerk's refusal to return the book have been an actionable matter at all in
  a mountain county, or a letter that simply went unanswered?
* While there: whether the store could be compelled to produce a **copy** rather than the original, because
  if the original leaves Tyler's desk for a term, Act Two has a hole in it and I want to decide that on
  purpose rather than find it in proofreading.

**What I will do if it cannot be found:** demote the exhibit to testimony — Tyler reads the entry aloud from
his own desk, which is dramatically weaker (no object in the room) but legally safe, and I would log the
change here so the trial chapter's staging reflects it.

## 3. Questions: direction, characters, style, voice

### Direction
**Q1. Is this a novel, a stage play, or a screenplay?** (See §1.1.) If it's a novel, this outline must
be re-cut into ~20 chapters with POV assignments, and the language of "images," "voiceover," and
"titles" translated or thrown out.

**Q2. Whose book is it?** The outline gives Jacob the most words but Caleb the most *action*, and
Jacob is offstage in prison for two acts. Three viable spines:
- **Caleb** (21 → 24): the classic education-into-violence arc; clean chronology; risk: he's the
  hothead, so the book becomes "young man learns nothing."
- **Joshua** (45): the only man who can stand in both worlds; his refusal is the book's spine and his
  compromise (helping Caleb) is its fall; he's the natural "engine of discovery" (Guide §2.1) because
  *he is the only one who knows the informant's name and must decide whether to say it*.
- **Margaret** (40s): the mother of the informer and wife of the prisoner, the only character for whom
  every act is a loss; currently the outline gives her 6 mentions against Parrish's 39.
My read: **Joshua primary (~80%), Margaret secondary (~20%)**, with Jacob and Caleb outside the head
entirely, so the reader is in the only mind that can see both sides and pay for it. Your call, and it
is the biggest one.

**Q3. What is the promise the community breaks?** Act One says the meeting resolves "if Parrish comes,
we'll handle it." Act Two shows them ambushing a deputy and Act Three an assassination. Is there a
moment where the cove *means* its "we're not murderers" and then un-means it — or is the collapse
already latent in the barn meeting? I'd want one written line at that meeting that Act Three violates.

**Q4. Where is the reader's hope?** A three-act tragedy currently stacked with raid, prison, maiming,
fire, manhunt, and prison. In *The Bound Tree* there is a whole Act Two "winter in the hollow" of
 competence and warmth. Here, Act One's cove is described in four short paragraphs and then never
 returned to. Do you want a set piece of happiness — a corn shucking, a wedding, a hunting trip, a
 night the still runs clean and everybody drinks — and would you grant the reader one scene where the
 trade is plainly *good*? Also: a romance (Caleb? Matthew?) is the usual lever. Keep or refuse?

**Q5. Does Jacob have to *do* anything in Act Two?** He currently writes a letter. Either the prison
thread gets its own engine (a warden, a fellow prisoner, a deal, a second raid ordered from inside,
Joshua visiting and being turned away) or Jacob must be deliberately made an absence that the book
feels. Right now he is merely parked.

**Q6. Do you want a fake-historiography frame at all?** The epilogue speaks in the voice of a scholar
("Caleb Brennan's fate is unknown," "historians aren't entirely sure"). That's a genre promise — the
found-document mode — and it's absent from *The Bound Tree*. Choose one: (a) no frame, no epilogue
voice, close on an image; (b) a frame, in which case we must write *real* history into it, correctly
cited, and it should be a written "author's note" rather than a titles card; (c) a frame that is
*ironic* — the epilogue is the invented dime-novel/newspaper version of Caleb, which is how the Redmond
story really was made. I'd take (c).

**Q7. What is the "revenue"?** Title is doing work you haven't cashed. Federal revenue? The cove's
revenue? Jacob's body of work? The railroad's? Parrish's career? I'd like the word to arrive four
different ways across the three acts, and I'd like to hear from you which is the last one.

**Q8. Act Two and Act Three are both titled "THE RECKONING."** Rename; and note "The Empty Year"
(Act Two opening) is the best heading in the document and the one act-title the book actually earns.

### Characters
**Q9. Whose son is Caleb?** He calls Jacob "Father" in the prison scene while the outline calls him the
nephew. If Jacob raised him (whose mother? which brother dead, and when, and of what), that's the best
unwritten backstory in the file — it's why a 21-year-old has more to lose than the man whose name is on
the still. Decide it, and decide whether the book ever says it plainly.

**Q10. Matthew's reason.** "He's 19, ambitious, and not entirely convinced of his father's
righteousness," plus cash and an exit ticket, is a thin motive for the single act that destroys four
lives. Options worth considering: he is trying to *save* Jacob from a worse outcome by taking a deal
before the stills are found (informed *for* his father); he has a child somewhere; he is in debt to
Hoss Tyler's store and Parrish bought the note; he's a Methodist convert and genuinely believes the
law is God's ordering; he hates being a Brennan. I want you to pick or to surprise me. Also: does
Matthew *take* the money, given R4?

**Q11. Parrish's cost.** He's excellent in Act One (courteous, blocked, needing a man on the inside)
and generic after that. Your Guide §1.3 says the antagonist must be reasonable *within the logic of
his institution* — so give him the institution's pressure: post-Whiskey-Ring Treasury (1875) meant
agents were being watched, supervisors fired, and moieties cut; the 1883 Civil Service reform abolished
the supervisors altogether. Is Parrish a reform believer who discovers enforcement is a numbers game?
Does his file get audited? Does anyone in Washington ever read his reports? Is the promoted man in the
final option a man who has lost a deputy to a squirrel rifle and never told his wife?

**Q12. Margaret.** 6 mentions. She is Jacob's wife, Matthew's mother, and — in Act Two — the mother of
a man who is not coming back. Is she a character or is she the thing the men are fighting about? If she
gets POV (Q2), what does she want, which is not what Jacob wants?

**Q13. Old Hoss Tyler appears exactly once** and he is the man who runs the "closed circuit" the book
is about to lose. The store is where the jug becomes flour, salt, and tobacco, and therefore the place
where a price and therefore a tax first becomes real. He is also the obvious first man Parrish buys.
Promote him or cut him?

**Q14. William Harper's barn must matter, and he's a stranger.** He gets one mention in a 3,300-word
document, and then the moral turn of Act Two hinges on him burning. Two fixes: (a) put Harper in the
Act One barn meeting — make him the one old man who says *don't*, and whom the others outvote; (b)
move the barn to something we've been in: Joshua's mill, or Jacob's own winter kitchen. I'd do both.

**Q15. The judge.** Do we see Robert P. Dick (R2) on the page, kindly, in his robes, and does he go
easy on Jacob, and does that *not help*?

### Style & voice
**Q16. Register.** Guide §5.3 bans eye dialect and says build voice from cadence and concrete nouns.
Concretely for this book: is the cove's speech **asyndetic and declarative, short, noun-heavy, no
subordination** ("He has a rifle. He has a badge."), and Parrish's **periodic, subordinate,
passive, Latinate** ("It is my understanding that the property in question…")? If yes, then the
outline's best lines are already right ("Right doesn't matter. They have guns and paper.") and its
weakest are the ones where the mountain men speak like essayists: "You sold him to the government for
money," "That's not the same. Once you cross into murder, there's no coming back," "Sometimes you
can't stop what's coming. You just have to survive it." Which do you want me to rewrite?

**Q17. The "not X — it's Y" tic.** Your Pass 4 explicitly bans it. In 3,345 outline words I count
**28 "not"**s, including 7 instances of the exact banned construction ("It's not criminal—it's just
what you do"; "He's not the first tax collector… but he's the first who's serious"; "It's not
gentle—they leave him…"; "who's not a distiller himself but…"; "The ambush is rough, not
well-planned—"; "He's not imprisoned—he never committed a direct crime—but…"), plus **8 "just"**s, **47
em-dashes**, and 11 "way"s. The outline is already producing the tic. Do you want me to hold the
draft to zero of these and let the *facts* carry the contrast, or keep a small number as deliberate
mountain rhetoric?

**Q18. Abstraction audit.** Pass 2 (over-explanation cut) has a lot of work here: "they have a moral
claim," "Something crystallizes in Caleb," "this is destruction, punishment, collective injury,"
"living a half-life between worlds," "a hollow victory," "the old economy is ending," "It's a closed
circuit." Which of these do you want converted into objects (my list: the ledger, the sold copper, the
loose mules, the nod) rather than asserted?

**Q19. Interiority vs. your outline's habit.** The outline explains feeling in exactly the way §2.3
forbids in scene: "Joshua is heartsick," "Joshua is tormented," "Caleb is furious," "He's trapped,"
"Matthew is tempted," "Parrish is alarmed and energized," "He's not proud, but he's not fully
repentant either." In the novel these must be gesture and withheld speech. Confirm: **interiority only
when alone, never in a live exchange**, and I will rewrite every one of those beats — including the
barn-burning crowd scene, which is currently narrated as Caleb's thesis statement.

**Q20. Period vocabulary for the men.** Guide §5.1 says verify by year. Two rulings I want from you
before drafting: (a) do we use **"moonshiner/moonshine"** in narration and dialogue at all, given that
in NC the men were first called **"blockaders"** and their product **"blockade whiskey"**, and
"moonshiner" only became common in the 1870s? I'd suggest the cove never calls itself anything (they
"run," they "make") and the *papers* call them moonshiners — which also makes the dime-novel naming a
theme. (b) **"revenuer"** vs. "revenue agent"/"inspector"/"gauge": who says which, and does Parrish
object to "revenuer" out loud, once?

**Q21. Scale and structure.** *The Bound Tree* is 20 chapters / ~93k words over twelve months. This
spans 1878–1881 — three years and five seasons — with a two-year imprisonment inside it. Do we go to
~28–30 chapters (roughly 130–140k) for the sweep, or hold at 20 and compress the timeline (e.g.,
autumn 1878 – spring 1881, and Jacob's sentence to fourteen months)? Also confirm the act division you
want, since the natural break is *the raid on the stills*, not the sentencing.

**Q22. Sentence music.** Do you want this book's landscape/periodic sentences weighted at **night**
(the still runs at night, the warning boy is posted at night, the barn burns at night, the ridge scene
is at night — the outline has four night set pieces already), and short declaratives for the day work?
"Legible difficulty" is your guide's phrase, but *moonshine is a nocturnal trade*, and the bimodal
rhythm (§3.3) hands you day/night as an organizing principle for free.

**Q23. Final image.** The outline offers three endings and picks none. There is a fourth available and
I think it's the book: **the first train to Elk Park, June 1882** — one year after Jacob stops
distilling, and the mountain loses in a day what the tax could not take in four years, and no one is
to blame for it. Do you want it?
