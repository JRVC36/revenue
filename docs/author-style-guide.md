Author Style & Craft Guide — Kevin Russell
A general craft, editorial, historical-verification, and production guide for nineteenth-century American and Appalachian literary historical fiction.


________________


1. Core Aesthetic: The Physical and Procedural Machinery
1.1 Work Is the Primary Metaphor
Prose in this register is at its strongest when rendering physical and procedural labor step by step: running a survey chain through timber, setting a fracture with splints and wet rawhide, forging iron at a charcoal hearth, scaling logs, curing meat, weaving, drafting a legal instrument, or laying black powder in rock.


* Rule: Never decorate a working scene with ornamental similes when the tools, materials, and bodily mechanics are more compelling than any metaphor.
* Rule: Reveal character, competence, and moral authority through how a person handles tools, animals, weather, and instruments.
1.2 Physical & Procedural Plot Engines
Never hang the stakes of a novel on an abstract or invented technicality. Build the plot engine out of concrete, verifiable nineteenth-century mechanisms where physical objects and human witnesses hold the leverage.
* Rule (Sightline Staging for Read Documents): Whenever a scene turns on a point-of-view character reading another person's small-hand ledger, warrant, receipt, or pocket memorandum in live action, physically stage the resting surface, light, and distance in feet or inches (e.g., an open book braced on an oak tailboard four feet away) so the sightline is mechanically earned.
1.3 Reasonable Antagonists
Avoid ranting or theatrical villains. The most effective historical antagonist is courteous, patient, well-mannered, and entirely reasonable within the logic of his institution. He is dangerous not because he is cruel for sport, but because he genuinely views a landscape, a trade, or a community as a schedule of yields, improvements, and liabilities.

2. Point of View & The Interiority Rule
2.1 Primary Spine with Strategic Secondary POV (~80 / 20 Split)
* Protect the Engine of Discovery: Anchor the narrative in a primary close-third point of view whose blind spots, assumptions, and gradual education drive the story's revelations.
* Keep the Human Cost Visible: Deploy a secondary point of view sparingly (~15–20% of the book, introduced early enough to establish voice and used when key characters are separated) so the people bearing the physical or legal consequences of the plot are never reduced to off-stage objects.
2.2 Differentiate POVs by Attention, Not Prose Style
Do not switch into a different stylistic costume or heavy vernacular when changing point of view. Keep the same sentence architecture, period discipline, and sensory density across the whole book. Distinguish characters by what they notice and how they view the world.


2.3 The Interiority Rule (Hard Law)
* When the POV character is alone: High interiority is welcome. Free indirect thought, memory, calculation, and quiet moral reckoning belong in solitude.
* When another person is in the room: Cut interior monologue. Dialogue, silence, physical gesture, and what is withheld must carry the scene.
* During a confrontation: Never pause a live exchange to explain what a character is feeling or why a remark landed hard. Let the reader infer the interior state from what the character says, what they refuse to answer, and what their hands are doing.
* No off-stage tactical omniscience: A close-third POV cannot narrate when or why an antagonist stationed men out of sight before the POV arrived; render only the riders or marks standing in the road when the POV reaches them.
* No narrator flash-forwards: Never break the present horizon of a scene with prophetic omniscience ("while he lived", "for the last time").

3. Diction, Sentence Rhythm & Readability
3.1 The Cut-or-Name Rule (Never Substitute a Longer Synonym)
First drafts naturally accumulate placeholder nouns (thing, place, matter, stuff, area) and light verbs (went, got, put, made).


* Rule: When revising a placeholder noun or light verb, either cut it or name the specific physical object or action (split hickory, puncheon boards, clay bowl of spring water, sourwood staff).
* Hard Ban: Never replace a plain Anglo-Saxon word with a longer Latinate synonym to sound more "literary" or to inflate a readability score.
   * Bad (Latinate inflation): "did not resemble a woman preparing to offer comfort" / "something anterior to pain" / "He surfaced from the dark."
   * Good (Plain and physical): "did not look like a woman about to comfort anybody" / "something that happened before pain" / "He came up out of the dark."
3.2 Where Difficulty Belongs (Legible vs. Opaque Difficulty)
* Legible difficulty (Welcome): Technical, historical, botanical, medical, and legal specificity. A reader will gladly work to follow a nineteenth-century trade, craft, or legal procedure when every physical object is shown clearly in action.
* Opaque difficulty (Cut): Sentences that keep the reader waiting for the grammatical subject, stacked participial phrases (-ing clauses), ballad-style predicate/object inversions ("Flat it shut", "Neither ear did he pick up") where plain subject-verb-object order hits harder, and abstract nouns that explain an image after the image has already landed.
3.3 Bimodal Sentence Rhythm
* Long periodic sentences (25–45 words) belong in landscape passages, historical sweep, and slow procedural rhythms.
* Short, clipped declaratives (4–12 words) belong at moments of physical crisis, pain, and moral shock.

4. The Five-Pass Revision Discipline
Pass 1: Expand by Event, Never Upholstery
When lengthening a draft or giving an act room to breathe, add events, complications, and physical procedures—never adjectives, weather filler, or restated internal reflections. Every scene must leave the characters' practical situation different from how it began.
Pass 2: The Over-Explanation & Thesis-Paragraph Cut
When a scene places two competing realities on the table—two ledgers, a map laid over lived ground, a debt paid in physical labor—cut the final sentence or paragraph that explains the moral to the reader. Let the objects make the argument and stop one beat earlier than feels safe.
Pass 3: The Interior Filter-Word Sweep
Search every chapter for cognitive and sensory filter words and cut or recast them into direct perception:


* Target words: thought, knew, understood, realized, noticed, saw, heard, felt, could see, could feel, could hear, remembered, was aware.
* Weak (Filtered): "He noticed that there were no birds in the clearing, and he felt a cold drop in his stomach."
* Strong (Direct): "There were no birds."
Pass 4: The Phrase-Tic & Formula Audit
Before finalizing a manuscript, run a corpus frequency check across all chapters to catch recurring syntactic or physical crutches:


* Rhetorical contrasts and tricolons: "Not X, not Y, just Z" / "It was not X; it was Y."
* Abstract intensifiers: "the particular [noun] of", "a kind of", "in a way that", "something that was not quite..."
* Repeated somatic beats: jaw-clenching ("not a muscle in his jaw moved"), releasing a breath a character did not know they were holding, nodding slowly, looking down at one's hands.
* Restraint-tag & trade-modifier bleed: When interiority is barred in live dialogue, cut external tone-policing tags ("said quietly", "said evenly", "said flatly") and cap trade/carpentry modifiers ("flat", "clean", "square", "neat") so they stay attached to timber, iron, and stone rather than padding ordinary verbs.
* Cross-scene quantity & mileage audit: Reconcile net weights, sack/bushel counts, coin totals, and road mileages reckoned from different starting points across scene breaks.
* Keep the two or three instances across the manuscript where the construction does real work; rewrite or cut the rest.
Pass 5: The Chapter-Ending Momentum Pass
Audit the closing 25 lines of every chapter. A chapter often drafts one paragraph past its true ending—adding a sleepy summary, a philosophical wrap-up, or a flash-forward that releases the dramatic pressure early.


* Rule: Cut the trailing release paragraph so every chapter ends on an active hook: a physical change in the room, an unanswered threat, a decision made in silence, or a ticking clock.

5. Nineteenth-Century Historical & Technical Verification
5.1 Verify Every Object, Law, and Table by Exact Year
Never rely on general nineteenth-century impressions; technology, law, and trade vocabulary shifted decade by decade in the 1800s. Verify dates and regional usage for every technical element:


* Instruments & Tools: Verify patent and adoption dates before naming specific hardware (e.g., open-vane horsehair sights vs. telescopic crosshairs; cant hooks vs. post-1858 peaveys; single-bit felling axes vs. later double-bitted axes; split shakes and slabs vs. tarpaper).
* Lighting, Fuel & Materials: Verify pre-petroleum fuels and materials for pre-1850s settings (whale oil, lard oil, tallow dips, and rosin vs. post-1854 kerosene or coal oil).
* Tables, Measures & Trade Rules: Verify which commercial tables, scales, or statutory rates were actually in print during the year of the story (e.g., 1825 Doyle and 1846 Scribner log rules vs. 1906 International rules).
* Statutes & Courthouses: Verify state-specific statutory timelines, witness requirements, county entry fees, and legal instruments for the exact decade and jurisdiction.
5.2 Maintain an Automated Anachronism Linter
Keep a strict, automated regex checker in the project repository that scans every chapter for:


1. Modern clinical or psychological vocabulary: trauma, psychological, adrenaline, hypothermia, compartment syndrome, arterial, subsonic, ecosystem. Replace with period-authentic physical descriptions ("the cold that comes before the sleep", "the blood choked off above the ankle").
2. Modern casual or business idioms: okay, percentage (use share or proportion), deadline (use term or sitting), bunkhouse (use crew shanty or quarters), karst (coined 1893; use limestone sink or swallow-hole).
3. Project-specific continuity anchors: injury sides, elapsed seasons, travel distances, and family relationships.
5.3 Regional & Indigenous Voice Without Caricature
* No eye-dialect spelling: Never misspell words ("wuz", "fer", "yer") to signal a mountain, rural, or Indigenous speaker.
* Build regional and cultural voice through cadence, grammar, economy, and worldview—letting a speaker's syntax, rhythm, and choice of concrete nouns reflect their trade, their language structure, and their relationship to the land.

6. Repository Architecture & Multi-Format Production Workflow
6.1 Repository Structure
Keep every book project self-contained in Git with zero external library dependencies (Python 3.9+ standard library only):

  canon-vN.md            Single source of truth: timeline, cast, technical/legal
                         mechanics, voice rules, and continuity rulings
docs/
  author-style-guide.md  Standing craft, editing, and production rules
  period-audit.md        Historical & technical verification log
  pitch-and-blurb.md     Logline, query hook, agent pitch, and jacket copy
  beta-reader-note.md    Reader margin guide and post-read questionnaire
manuscript/
  01-chapter-slug.md     One canonical Markdown file per chapter
tools/
  compile.py             Builds Markdown, typeset HTML, and Kokoro TTS text
  check.py               Strict anachronism and continuity linter
  readability.py         Narration-only sentence and syllable diagnostics
dist/                    Compiled outputs (gitignored; synced to Google Drive)

Precedence Rule: Whenever an earlier outline or bible conflicts with a choice that works on the page, update canon/ to match the manuscript.
6.2 Human-Reader Typeset Build (dist/*.html → Google Docs)
When generating HTML for conversion to Google Docs and human reading:


* Font Declaration: Always write font-family: Georgia; without single quotes or , serif fallbacks (Google Drive's HTML importer misparses 'Georgia', serif into the sans-serif web font Geo). Follow upload with a document-wide updateTextStyle setting weightedFontFamily to Georgia.
* Page Architecture: Include a centered Title Page (with useFirstPageHeaderFooter: true so the running header begins after Page 1), a Contents page grouped by Act, dedicated Act Divider Pages (Heading 1, centered with 170pt top drop-margin), and Chapter Drop-Heads (Heading 2, centered with 48pt top margin and 24pt bottom margin) so the Google Docs Left Outline sidebar forms a clean navigable tree.
* Native Page Breaks: Insert a placeholder token ([[PAGE_BREAK]]) before every major section in HTML and convert each token in reverse index order into a true Google Docs insertPageBreak element via the Docs API.
* Book Indentation & Scene Breaks: Set the first paragraph of every chapter and the first paragraph after every scene break flush-left (text-indent: 0pt); set all subsequent body paragraphs to 21.6pt (0.3") first-line indent at 12pt Georgia, 1.38 line spacing. Render scene breaks (---) as centered book asterisms (*   *   *), never full-width horizontal lines.
6.3 Audiobook / Kokoro TTS Build (dist/*-audiobook.txt)
When generating the plain-text script for Kokoro TTS (misaki / espeak-ng):


1. Eliminate all scene-break lines (---) and Markdown symbols (#, *, `): Replace scene breaks with clean paragraph breaks and omit the visual Table of Contents.
2. Convert all em-dashes (—) and en-dashes (–) by syntactic context:
   * Interrupted dialogue before a lowercase speech tag ("I—" he said.) → comma ("I," he said.).
   * Interrupted dialogue before a new sentence ("Wait—" He turned.) → period (.").
   * Mid-sentence parenthetical or appositive em-dashes → commas (, ) with duplicate adjacent punctuation collapsed.
   * Date or season en-dashes (October – November) → spoken "to".
3. Remove non-Latin script blocks and normalize single-letter glosses: Strip inline Unicode glyphs that English G2P cannot phonemize, and spell single-letter vowel/syllable lessons phonetically ("Ah.", "Nah.", "the letter D") so they are never read as the English indefinite article "uh".
4. Eliminate all internal abbreviation periods and raw digits:
   * Expand Mr. → Mister, Mrs. → Missus, Dr. → Doctor, Col. → Colonel, No. → Number, est. → estimated, ft. → feet, & → and, and space out initials without periods (J R, A S) so two-letter initials are never tokenized as words or acronyms and downstream TTS sentence splitters never cut a sentence mid-clause.
   * Spell out all Roman numerals (Act One, Act Two, Act Three), chapter numbers (Chapter One. through Chapter Twenty.), document numbers, and four-digit years (eighteen forty-eight) so zero digits (0–9) remain in the file.
