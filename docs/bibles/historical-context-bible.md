## 1. The tax, precisely

| Enacted / effective | Rate on distilled spirits |
|---|---|
| Act of Jul 1, 1862 | 20¢ per proof gallon |
| Mar 7, 1864 | 60¢ |
| Jul 1, 1864 | $1.50 |
| Jan 1, 1865 | $2.00 |
| Jul 20, 1868 | 50¢ |
| Jun 6, 1872 | 70¢ |
| **Mar 3, 1875 → Aug 27, 1894** | **90¢ ← the whole book lives here** |

**Source:** TTB, *Historical Tax Rates*, Table 1. A period source writing in 1881 confirms the 70→90¢
increase in March 1875 and that it "has remained up to the date of the present writing."

A **proof gallon** is one gallon at 100 proof. Spirits at 120 proof pay 1.2 gal of tax per gallon
sold. This distinction is worth one sentence of explanation on the page, in a character's mouth, once.

**The arithmetic that justifies the plot.** A bushel of corn marketed at roughly 25¢ yields about 2½
gallons of whiskey worth roughly $1.25. **The tax is approximately the entire value of the whiskey.**
That is the 1791 grievance re-created in 1878, and it is why the cove's position is not ignorance but
arithmetic. Never argue it. A man counts on his fingers and the reader does the rest.

Other levies in the same system, if needed for a scene: special taxes on retail dealers and
rectifiers; a bonded-warehouse regime; gauging at the warehouse; the collector's, supervisor's, and
inspector's separate paperwork. **UNVERIFIED at the level of exact 1878 dollar amounts.**

## 2. The law that actually bites (R-3)

Three separate authorities, all in force 1878–1881:

1. **Act of July 20, 1868**, ch. 186, 15 Stat. 125 — the Reconstruction-era redesign of the spirits
   tax. Includes: no still, boiler, or vessel for distilling **in any dwelling-house, or in any shed,
   yard, or enclosure connected with one**; a distiller's bond with at least two sureties; **no bond
   approved unless the distiller is owner in fee, unencumbered by mortgage, judgment, or other lien**,
   of the land the distillery stands on, unless every lienholder consents in writing, acknowledged,
   stipulating that the United States's lien for taxes and penalties has **priority**; forfeiture of
   the distillery, apparatus, **and all real estate and premises connected therewith**; a fine of
   $500–$5,000 and imprisonment of 6 months to 2 years; an assessor who approves a bond out of order
   forfeits $2,000 and is dismissed.
2. **Revised Statutes** §§ 3258 (unregistered still forfeited, with all personal property in the
   building or connected yard), 3281 (carrying on the business of a distiller without bond or with
   intent to defraud: $1,000–$5,000, 6 months–2 years).
3. **Act of February 8, 1875**, ch. 36, § 16, 18 Stat. 310 — re-enacts and expands the forfeiture to:
   spirits, stills, and apparatus **wherever found**; spirits and personal property in the distillery
   or any connected building, room, yard, or inclosure; the distiller's interest in the land; **and the
   interest of "every person who knowingly has suffered or permitted the business of a distiller to be
   there carried on, or has connived at the same."**

Case law that will show up if the author wants the trial chapter to be real:
- **Dobbins' Distillery v. United States**, 96 U.S. 395 (1878) — a **landlord's** distillery forfeited
  by a tenant's fraud **unknown to the landlord**. Decided the year the book opens.
- **United States v. Stowell**, 133 U.S. 1 (1890) — retrospective construction of the same regime;
  forfeiture reaches personal property a stranger knowingly left on the premises, but reaches land only
  as against the distiller and those who consented.
- Act of Jun 6, 1872 (12th section) — the provision cutting off informer shares not already fixed and
  paid; see §4.

**Consequence for the book.** The government's case is *in rem*: the libel names the property, not the
man. The question at issue is **knowledge and consent**, not guilt. Every scene in Acts Two and Three
should be, underneath, about who saw what and whether they said a word about it.

## 3. Who enforces it, and how much he is paid

- **Bureau of Internal Revenue**, Treasury Department. **Commissioner: Green B. Raum**, appointed by
  Grant in August 1876; he stayed through the 1880s, and NC accounts credit him with both the
  crackdown on western NC stills beginning **1878** (the contemporaneous term is a "moonshine war")
  and, later, relaxing the persecution of moonshiners.
- **Supervisors of internal revenue** for each district (a Bureau expansion of the early 1870s);
  abolished by the **Pendleton Civil Service Act of 1883**. Parrish's career sits inside the life of
  that office.
- **Assessors, collectors, inspectors, gaugers, deputy collectors.** **Deputy marshals** were hired by
  the U.S. Marshal for the district, often paid per prisoner — which is why a raiding party looks like
  hired men, and why a "posse" is not a militia.
- **Western District of North Carolina**, created June 4, 1872 (17 Stat.). First judge:
  **Robert Paine Dick** (1823–1898), a Greensboro lawyer, ex-Confederate legislator who could not take
  the ironclad oath, former NC Supreme Court justice, a Grant appointee — remembered in his biography
  for his "kindly temper in administrating the internal revenue laws." Division seats today are
  Asheville, Statesville, Charlotte; **AVERY County and WATAUGA County sit in different modern
  divisions (Asheville / Statesville), which is a useful hint about where the 1870s court went.**
  A federal courthouse building in Asheville dates to **1892**; before that, terms sat in whatever
  rooms the town had. **UNVERIFIED: exact term dates and seats 1878–1881 — audit item A-2.**
- **Casualties.** U.S. Marshals Service's own roll for the "Moonshine War, 1872–1913" lists a Deputy
  Marshal **Burns, shot in the back from ambush, April 13, 1874, Western District of North Carolina**,
  and **C. B. Brockus, killed February 14, 1893, WDNC, during a prison rescue attempt** — real, local,
  and on either side of this book.
- **Scale of violence, honestly.** Bruce E. Stewart counts roughly **25 agents killed in the line of
  duty between 1876 and 1880** in the mountain South (about six a year), against roughly twenty a year
  for deputy marshals out West; the national coverage blew mountain killings out of proportion. **Use
  this to deflate, not to inflate, Act Three.**

## 4. Informers: the money question

Under the 1864 and 1865 acts, the person who first informed of an offense took **one moiety** (one
half) of fines, penalties, and forfeitures. That produced exactly the abuses one would expect —
including an 1870s episode where a collector's own informer share had to be litigated up to the
Supreme Court (**Dorsheimer v. United States**, 74 U.S. 166 (1869)).

**The **Act of June 6, 1872** cut this off: moieties were repealed and replaced by discretionary
payments, "not exceeding in the aggregate the amount appropriated therefor," at the judgment of the
Commissioner with the Secretary of the Treasury's approval — with an earlier formulation capping an
informer at one moiety and $5,000 in any one case. In the reported litigation over it, a claim failed
because the fine had not been both fixed **and paid** before August 1, 1872.

**For the manuscript (Q-7):** the informers' market is gone, or at least it is a favor. Parrish
promising Matthew a percentage is a lie by omission, and Matthew's payoff — if it comes — is a
bureaucratic act performed by a man in Washington who has never smelled the hollow. That is the period
truth and it is a better scene than the outline's "good money."

## 5. Politics in the mountains, 1878–1882 (canon §7's open question)

What the region is simultaneously living through, any of which the author may admit or deliberately
exclude:
- **The memory of the liquor tax as Reconstruction.** The Enforcement Acts (1870–71) gave the federal
  government the same tools for the Klan and for the still; in North Carolina that meant the
  **Kirk–Holden war (1870)**, Colonel George Kirk's militia in Alamance and Caswell, Governor Holden's
  impeachment and acquittal (1871–72), and — for the mountains — Klan violence aimed partly at revenue
  men and Black officeholders in Rutherford and McDowell counties (1868–72). The real slogan of the
  period, from Stewart's chapter title: **"They tax us and give us Negro civil rights."**
- **Zebulon B. Vance**, elected governor 1876 over Republican Thomas Settle on a fusion with the
  Greenbackers, then to the U.S. Senate in 1879. Mountain farmers' politics in 1878 are Vance's
  politics: debt, taxes, and paper money.
- **The state's own revenue fight.** North Carolina's postwar debt — the 1868 "funded" and "unfunded"
  railroad bonds, the "readjustment" battle, taxes cut and restored to pay bondholders — is the state
  analogue of Parrish's business. A NC reader in 1878 has been arguing about revenue for a decade.
- **Black enfranchisement and its overthrow.** County offices, the 1868 constitution's schools, and
  the 1898 Wilmington coup far ahead. In 1878 there are Black officeholders and Republican sheriffs in
  the North Carolina Piedmont; there are almost none in the high coves, and the reason is worth one
  line.
- **The war itself, fifteen years back.** Bridge-burners of Nov 1861 (Yancey/Madison), the Confederate
  Home Guard, the **Shelton Laurel massacre** (Jan 1863, Madison County — thirteen Unionists shot after
  summary trial), deserters and bushwhackers in the coves around Grandfather Mountain. The men aged
  45–50 in 1878 were of age in 1862–64. **No character in this book is too old to have a war record,
  and every man in the cove has one.**

## 6. The economy, and what the railroad does to it

**Cranberry before the rails** (`USER`, Drive research doc): subsistence yeoman farms; corn, buckwheat,
rye; hogs, cattle, sheep; log cabins; grist mills; Baptist and Methodist meeting houses; cash scarce,
trade by barter; everything hauled by wagon to Morganton, NC or Elizabethton, TN over the Yellow
Mountain Turnpike. Population of the Cranberry district ~200–400; the whole of Burke County 9,000–10,000
in 1850–60. Slavery minimal; a non-plantation, free-labor society; the mountains largely Whig, then
Unionist.

**Iron, always the counter-story.** The Cranberry magnetite bed — one of the largest and purest deposits
in the eastern U.S. (up to 68% iron), known to the Cherokee before removal, a forge on the Elk River by
1826, worked intermittently in the 1840s–50s because hauling cost more than the ore was worth. Clingman
and Perkins among the speculators. Confederate interest in 1861–62 and bloomery output for the ordnance
works, hauled down the mountain as bars dragged over dirt paths. **The mine is why the train comes.**

**The railroad, and the ruling in canon R-2.**
- Chartered by the Tennessee legislature **May 24, 1866** (originally 5-ft gauge) to reach the Cranberry
  mines. Five miles of track by April 1870; abandoned in **1874**.
- Bought through the **Cranberry Iron & Coal Company**, controlled by **Ario Pardee** of Pennsylvania;
  purchase date reported variously as 1873, 1875, and 1876–79 (**UNVERIFIED: audit A-6**).
- Reorganized as a **3-foot narrow gauge** by an act of **March 6, 1879**; construction under way from
  **1879–1880**; engineer **Thomas Matson**; gradients to 4%; rock blasted by hand with black powder.
- **Johnson City → Hampton, TN (14.1 mi): completed August 22, 1881.**
- **Into North Carolina at State Line Gap; Elk Park reached mid-June 1882; Cranberry July 3, 1882.**
  Cost about $1 million. **Elk Park was the first NC stop and became the biggest town in the county.**
- Company town at Cranberry: company houses, company hotel, company store and scrip; later a school and
  clinic. **Elk Park one mile down the track as the unregulated twin**: hotels, boarding houses,
  saloons, stables, mercantiles — fed by miners' wages. Telegraph in **1883**.
- After 1882: travel across the Unaka to Johnson City in hours instead of days; mass-produced cloth,
  cast stoves, canned goods, and northern tools on the store shelves; farm produce, tanbark, and timber
  leaving by boxcar; young mountaineers leaving subsistence farming for track crews, mines, and steam
  band sawmills.

**Why this matters to the book's argument.** The tax was never going to end the cove's way of life;
ninety cents a gallon against a $1.25 bushel economy is a nuisance the mountain could outlast. The
connection ended it — by giving a man a better price for his corn than the still could, and by
bringing in men who did not live in the hollow and did not need its consent. Parrish wins a small
victory in 1881 and the market wins everything in 1882. **That is the ending, and it is free.**

## 7. Religion, schooling, lawlessness

- **Churches:** Baptist (anti-missionary/regular and missionary splits), Methodist circuit riders;
  meeting houses as the community center; funerals and weddings as the only assemblies. `USER`
- **Schooling:** sparse. A county academy or two; the 1868 constitution's public-school system was new,
  distrusted, and separately funded; literacy is high enough to be interesting and low enough that a
  deed is a foreign document. **Do not write a cove that cannot read; write a cove that does not trust
  writing.** That is the whole legal engine of the book.
- **Violence baseline:** the Reconstruction-era mountain county courthouse, whiskey, land titles, and
  the famous 1881 killing of a sheriff in the aisle at Wilkesboro (the Ashe County murder trial — later
  venue-shifted to Raleigh for fear of armed rescues) is period-plausible texture. **UNVERIFIED in
  detail; do not use.**

## 9. What must not be said

- **"Whiskey Rebellion"** for 1878–81. That name belongs to 1791–94 in western Pennsylvania.
  Contemporaries and the scholarship say **"moonshine war"** or **"whiskey war."**
- **The Whiskey Ring** is not a moonshine story. St. Louis, Chicago, Milwaukee, 1871–76: bonded
  distilleries bribing gaugers to under-report, ~$0.35/gal to stamp whiskey as taxed at the legal rate,
  238 indicted, 110 convicted, Grant's deposition for Babcock (the first by a sitting president in a
  criminal trial). **It belongs in this book only as the reason Washington is serious** — and it should
  appear as a man in a Washington office refusing to be the kind of agent who takes a gift.
- No locomotive west of the Blue Ridge before June 1882.
- No Avery County before 1911; no "Avery" in a character's mouth at all.
- No "IRS." **Bureau of Internal Revenue**, or "the revenue," or "the Department."
- No "moonshiner" from a mountaineer's own mouth (see Q-14 and the vocabulary bible).
- No "Reconstruction" as a self-conscious label in mountain speech. They would name the years, not the
  period.
