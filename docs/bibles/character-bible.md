# Character Bible — *REVENUE*

**Status: v0.9 — open.** Companion to `canon/canon-v1.md` §4. Ages, dates, and relationships are
recorded there and are authoritative; this file is for interior material — what a person wants, what a
person will not say, and what their hands do.

House rule that governs every entry (Style Guide §2.2): **POVs are differentiated by attention, not by
prose style.** Same sentence architecture for everyone. What changes is what each person notices.

---

## Jacob Brennan · b. ~1833 · 45 in 1878 · distiller · the younger brother (R-6) · sitting on his wife's land (R-7)

**Notices:** water, grade, drainage, the sound a fire makes when the wood is wet. Weather as arithmetic,
not mood. He reads a man by what the man does with his hands when he thinks no one is pricing him.
**Doesn't notice:** his own son's ambition, until it is a decision.
**Wants:** to be left alone *with* his neighbors — the distinction matters; he does not want
independence, he wants the cove's consent.
**Will not say:** that he knows the still's location is itself the offense, and chose it anyway.
**Signature action:** fixes something that is not broken before he leaves a room.
**Engine hook (§canon R-3 + R-6 + R-7):** he owns nothing the libel can reach, which is why he is calm in
Act One. The tract in the libel is **his wife's** — and the encumbered farm next door is his elder brother's. Every scene of his should still have the ground
under it — literally — but the ground belongs to Joshua, and that is the fact Jacob never says out loud
because saying it would mean admitting the cost of his own competence falls on somebody else.
**Legal fact he cannot use:** he cannot register. Bond approval required an owner in fee *unencumbered*,
and where there was a lien, every lienholder had to consent in writing — so the only lawful road ran
through the mortgage holder's signature. One flat line from the miller in Act One, then never explained.
**1862–65 (ruled: canon R-16):** did not enlist on either side; stayed on the mountain cutting and hauling
around Cranberry Forge enough to stay off early conscript lists and lying out in the laurel when the Home
Guard swept the creek — keeping the place and five-year-old Caleb for the three years Joshua was in the Union
army, which is how the boy came to call him "Father" (R-8).
**Cost he pays:** he is remembered as a man who did nothing.

## Margaret Brennan · 40s in 1878 · wife; mother of the informer

**Notices:** what a room will cost to put back. Who has eaten. Which jug is already missed.
**Wants:** the paperwork. She is the only character who reads the notices posted at the courthouse,
because the notices are against her freehold — a woman who has spent her life being the reason a debt could
not reach a family is suddenly the defendant in a case about her own dirt.
**Will not say:** that she knew before the raid. Under R-7 this is no longer atmosphere: *Stowell* protects
an owner who did not know and *Dobbins* does not protect one who merely did not ask, so whether Margaret
knew is the single fact the trial turns on and she is the only witness who can supply it. **Locked in
`docs/act-1-beat-sheet.md` (Ch. 8, *The Cold Still*):** she knew everything a woman forty paces upwind knows
by household arithmetic, meal weights, and singlings-sour laundry — and kept strict boundary discipline
(never crossing the barn sill, never touching a jug, never executing a written lease or privy examination
under N.C. Laws 1848–49 ch. 41), pricing her husband's trade against forty acres she believed `Art. X §6`
put beyond reach until the federal *in rem* notice landed on her doorpost.
**Signature action:** keeps the household's accounts in a book nobody else can read, and it turns out to
be evidence.
**Raised him (R-8).** She is the woman who took in her husband's nephew at five years old and did not
hand him back after the war, which is the only act of refusal available to a woman with land and no
attorney. Nobody in the book thanks her for it and nobody mentions it.
**Engine hook (ruled: canon R-7 + R-20):** she owns the ground the still stands on — her father's tract,
held in fee as a married woman's separate estate (`NC Const. 1868 Art. X §6`), with the folded pre-1861
Watauga root deed in her cedar box the sole surviving copy after the 23 March 1873 Boone courthouse fire
(R-15). Under N.C. Laws 1848–49 ch. 41 and *Scott v. Battle*, 85 N.C. 184 (1881), neither a husband's lease
nor implied consent/estoppel could divest or charge a married woman's separate realty without a certified
privy examination. In *Did She Know?* (Ch. 26, R-20), she lays down that unburned deed, stands on the total
absence of any clerk's privy-examination certificate giving Jacob or any man an interest in her barn, and in
her one sentence on knowledge refuses to clear `Parcel B` by naming Caleb or Joshua — stalling the federal
decree into *E1*'s `"the tract remains in contest"` (R-12) while mortgage and defense costs still force
Joshua to sell the mill in Ch. 27.
**Currently in the outline:** 6 mentions against Parrish's 39, and she has one good scene (the nod in
the courthouse). That scene is the seed.
**Ruled (canon R-4): she is the ~20% secondary POV.** Her chapters are the ones where *paper* decides
something — the posted notices, the search of the house, her own standing at the term. She does not get
the grief chapters and she never gets a scene of her crying alone; her interiority is arithmetic and
inventory, and it is allowed to include what a room costs to put back.

## Matthew Brennan · 19 in 1878 (b. ~1859) · Jacob's son · the informant · raised as Caleb's brother (R-8) · three asks (R-10)

**Notices:** prices, and other men's boots. He is the only character who looks at a man's shoes and
knows what the man earns.
**Wants:** to be elsewhere, and to be forgiven before he has done anything.
**R-8's addition, and it is the whole of him:** he grew up two years behind a boy who was not his brother by
blood and was his brother in every other way. When Caleb comes for him in Johnson City it is not the cove
collecting a debt; it is the family doing what families do. Write the beating as a family affair and refuse
the word.
**Will not say:** that he went to Parrish first. He will let every man in the cove believe he was
approached, because the truth makes him the author instead of the instrument.
**Signature action:** calculates in his head out loud, then stops when he notices someone watching.
**Engine hook (§canon 5.5):** from 1 Aug 1872 informer shares were **discretionary payments out of
Washington**. He burns his father for a check that a clerk decides about. The outline gives him
"money and a chance to leave"; consider instead a **letter**, months late, with an amount in it that
is less than what a season's corn brings. That is period-exact, it is funnier, and it is worse.
**Ruled (canon R-10): the store, the day, the paper — granted, granted, refused.** He goes to Parrish
about a debt at Tyler's book; he asks that the raid be set on a day he names; he asks for a written
appointment of any kind. Two are given, the third is declined courteously, and the day he picked gets a
fifteen-year-old shot behind Harper's barn. He is never again the boy who wanted out; he is the man whose
name appears in three documents and in no conversation.
**How to write a motive with no interiority (R-4 says he never gets a head):** three objects, no thoughts. A
line in a day book in Tyler's own hand (`"by order of the agent"`, R-10/R-11 — omitted from Parrish's public
exhibit transcript in *The Missing Page*, R-18). A date said once, in the store, and kept exactly. A letter
of appointment that is never written, described by Parrish as a difficulty of the service. If the scene needs
more than that, the scene is the wrong scene.

## Caleb Brennan · 21 in 1878 (b. ~1857) · Jacob's nephew, Joshua's firstborn (R-8) · calls Jacob "Father"

**Notices:** ground that hides a man. Positions. Who is unarmed. He is the only character whose
attention is already tactical, which is why he becomes the danger.
**Wants:** Jacob to be proud of the thing he is doing instead of the thing he is.
**Will not say:** that he understood Jacob's letter and agreed with it. He reads it twice and says
nothing — the outline already has "he doesn't argue, but he doesn't agree either." Keep exactly that
beat and refuse the interior gloss after it.
**Signature action:** cleans a weapon while other men talk; the talking decides nothing, the cleaning
decides everything.
**Arc problem to solve:** as outlined he is a hothead who learns nothing. Fix by making him the one who
*does* learn — who builds the warning system, who forbids killing Matthew and keeps that promise, and
who then breaks his own rule at the barn fire. The tragedy is not rage. It is a competent man arriving
at the wrong conclusion by a sound path.
**Ruled (canon R-8): he is Joshua's son, given to Jacob's house in 1862 when Joshua enlisted.** Every
outline label stays literally true — a Brennan, a nephew, "Father" — and the reason he has more at stake
than the man whose name is on the still is that he is heir to two properties and to neither.
**How to play it:** he does not know. Or he has known since he was eight and it is a fact he carries without
handling it, the way he carries a rifle. Do not choose the second; choose the first, and let the reader
supply that the second is also true. His rage at the burned barn is a boy defending the only household he has
ever been told is his, which is why the cove can trust him and why that trust is the tragedy.

## Joshua Brennan · b. ~1828 · 50 in 1878 · miller, trader · elder brother, owner in fee (R-6)

**Notices:** ledgers, weights, water-power, and the credit terms of everyone in the cove — he is the
only man in the book who knows what everything costs including loyalty.
**Wants:** the two facts in his life to stop being incompatible: that his brother is right, and that
the government has guns and paper.
**Will not say:** his refusal in Act One was also arithmetic. He is a bondman in all but name; the
mortgage is the reason his "no" is expensive and Jacob's is not.
**Signature action:** measures. He weighs the corn, he measures the toll, he measures how much he can
carry before he is the man who carried it too far.
**R-8 is the fact about him that the book never says.** His firstborn is the second son in his brother's
house; the boy's mother died in 1862 in the same childbed that took the second child; Joshua went to war
that summer and came back to find the arrangement permanent. Everything Joshua does in the book — the
refusal to inform, the sheltering of the fugitive, the sale of the mill, the leaving — is one continuous act
about that boy, and no sentence in the manuscript is permitted to say so. The three planted facts are in
canon §10 ("the Caleb audit"); the fourth is Joshua's silence when someone calls Caleb "your brother's boy"
and does not correct himself.
**What R-6 and R-18 did to him:** he inherited the farm and mortgaged it, so the still sits in the barn on
Margaret's adjoining freehold (corrected by R-7), and the forfeiture R-3 aims at the land reaches the POV
character's own deed via "connected therewith." He is also the one man in the cove who cannot plead not
knowing — an owner in fee is presumed to know what happens on connected premises, which is the doctrine, not a
metaphor. In *The Missing Page* (Ch. 15, R-18), Parrish puts that exact *Stowell* question to him over a
certified transcript of Tyler's seized day book, and Joshua spots the skipped folio number where Matthew's
settled store account (`"by order of the agent"`) was omitted from the public exhibit. His whole social
function is to hold the hollow's secret; his legal position is that the secret is his.
**What R-16 put in his drawer:** in summer **1862**, after Elsie died, he walked over State Line Gap into East
Tennessee and served three years in the **Union** army, mustering out at **Knoxville in 1865** (unit unnamed on
the page). Congress enacted the distilled-spirits tax that same summer to pay his army. Seventeen years later
the United States comes for his deed under the law that fed him, and he never once says so — his folded 1865
discharge sits beside the mortgage receipt in *What a No Costs* and does all the talking.
**Spine POV, ~80% (ruled: canon R-4).** He can enter both worlds, he is the only one who knows the
informant's name and must decide whether to say it, his refusal is the book's thesis, and his
compromise — sheltering Caleb — is the book's fall. His "gradual education" is the discovery engine the
Style Guide §2.1 requires, and Jacob's and Caleb's do not have one.
**What that costs him on the page:** he is the POV, so the book's one unaffordable sentence is his — the
reader must not be allowed to hear him call what he does *reasonable*.
**Ends:** sells the mill, leaves, is the only Brennan who dies in a town with a street name.

## William Parrish · 35–40 in 1878 · federal revenue agent

**Notices:** records, hours, and who is not in the room. He notices an empty chair the way a soldier
notices a gap in a wall.
**Wants:** the district's numbers to be clean. Not rich, not feared — *clean*. The post-Whiskey-Ring
Bureau needed agents who convicted without buying anyone, and he has been told, in writing, what kind
of agent he is not.
**Will not say:** that he is disappointed when Jacob is convicted. He wanted to be let in, and he
wanted it personally, and he will not examine that.
**Signature action:** writes in a fair hand while a man is talking about his livelihood; the
book-closing; he never takes notes on a man's face.
**Reasonable-antagonist discipline (§1.3):** he is courteous, patient, and correct within his
institution. He does not burn the barn. He also does not stop them, and the missing written order is
the most eloquent page in Act Two.
**His cost (ruled: canon R-9) is that the order becomes an exhibit in his own defense.** The Department
writes to ask why a raid was fought with hired men and why a barn the United States had a claim on was
burned. Both honest answers ruin him — *I did not order it* ends his authority; *it was necessary* admits
the government destroyed property it was seizing — and he writes the second one in that same fair hand.
The complaint closes without adverse finding, the promotion is a footnote, and the last thing he does in the
book is shut a file.
**The document he has to produce is a woman's word (*The Deposition*, ch. 23, R-19):** he needs an affidavit
that the fire at Harper's storage barn was not ordered by him, and the only witness whose oath Washington
cannot dismiss as a deputy covering a superior is Margaret — which is how he ends up asking the woman he is
dispossessing to save his career. Before signing, she makes him strike `"a boy"` from his Commissioner's
report and spell **`Elisha Councill`** (*E1*, R-10/R-12); then she swears one true sentence — that Parrish
stood in the road calling his hired possemen off when the blaze caught in the split kegs of blockade whiskey
stored on Harper's floor. It saves him with Washington, puts sworn proof of stored spirits into the federal
record against the cove, and neither of them calls it a favor.
**The folio he leaves out (*The Missing Page*, ch. 15, R-18):** when he puts the *Stowell* "connected
premises" question to Joshua over a certified exhibit transcript of Hoss Tyler's seized day book, he omits the
single folio carrying Matthew's settled store account (`"by order of the agent"`, R-10/R-11) so an irregular
pre-allowance payment to an informant never enters the public forfeiture record. Joshua spots the skipped
folio number in silence.
**Never:** the killed deputy named Burns — real, 1874, WDNC. He may *cite* Burns once, the way a man cites a
precedent: *Burns's file was thinner than mine will be.* That is the only interiority he is allowed.
**Never lets him:** rant. If he is angry, his handwriting gets smaller.

## Old Hoss Tyler · 63 in 1878 (b. ~1815) · the store · a creditor, not a Judas (R-11)

**Notices:** what a man will trade for salt in March versus October; the season's credit and the exact
moment it goes bad. He is the market inside the barter world, and therefore the hinge.
**Ruled (canon R-11): he is promoted and he is not bought.** Parrish's first acquisition is not an
informant, it is **a book** — the day book — and it is taken, not purchased. Tyler's response is the
mechanism the book needs: he protests the taking in writing, and his protest is what authenticates the
entries. A creditor protecting his property does the government's work for it, and does it with a straight
face, because an entry made the day it happened is the difference between a debt and a grudge.
**He is paid, which is not the same as corrupt:** the agent settles Matthew's debt with him at par, in coin,
on the order of the United States. He is the only man in the hollow who collects twice on the same boy — once
from the family, once from the government's willingness to spend public money to make a paper point.
**Will not say:** that he has been owed money by every family in the cove and that the government, unlike
them, can pay. He says this only in the way a man says it: by extending three of them and not the others.

## William Harper · 60 in 1879 (b. ~1819) · the barn · the man who said *don't* (R-11)

**Notices:** standing timber, and what a structure means as a years-long act. His barn is a document.
**Function under R-3:** he is the *knowledgeable bystander*. The government does not need to prove he
distilled. It needs to prove he knew and let it happen — and the whole community's discipline of
not-knowing is now a legal technique, not a moral virtue. He should have spent Act One learning how to
not know.
**Ruled (canon R-11): he is in the Act One meeting at Joshua's mill, and he says *don't*, and he is
outvoted.** One sentence — short enough that Act Two can hand it back to him, and it will be quoted verbatim
exactly once, in the chapter where his barn is burning. He does not say "I told you so"; the cove does not
get to hear that sentence, and neither does the reader.
**The burning is then doubly a crime:** it destroys what the libel would have had to win in court, and it is
done to the one neighbor who was right. Being right is not a defense in this book; it is only a forecast.
**His body language for the whole act:** a doorway, standing, sons behind him.

## The cove as a character

Rules for crowd scenes: the cove never votes on camera twice for the same thing (Act One's meeting
resists; Act Two's silence convicts). The **nod** is the community's full vocabulary of allegiance —
Jacob's porch ending and Margaret's courthouse ending are the same gesture at two ends of the book.
Establish it in Act One, in a scene where it means agreement, so the final nods mean nothing, and the
one time a man will not nod is the hinge of the book.

## Naming decisions (recorded here, enforced in canon)

- **Brennan** — `INVENT`. Not a period-typical Toe River / Cranberry name; that is a feature (they came in),
  but see the surname note in `vocabulary-tone-bible.md`. For the secondary cast, use attested **Cranberry
  Township (Mitchell) and Watauga-ridge names** (R-15) — Winters, Buchanan, Wiseman, Pritchard, Burleson,
  Gouge, Dugger, Banner, Avery, Henson, Calloway, Brown, Pressley, Coffey, Childress, Blevins, Councill — so
  the 1880 census reads true.
- **Elk Cove / North Fork Hollow** — the outline's candidates. `INVENT`, set as side hollows above the Elk
  River / Cranberry Creek drainage inside **`Cranberry Township, Mitchell County`** (R-15; distinct from the
  real Elk Park clearing one mile north of Cranberry Forge).
- **"The Whistlers"** — `RULED (R-14)`: owned invention, **print-only**. The name belongs to the Bureau's
  reports, the county paper, and the dime novel; not one mountain mouth ever says it, because to name the
  meeting is to prove it. Old Councill gets the closest thing the cove says about itself, and it is a story
  about somebody else's great-grandfather correcting an officer, not a banner. No documented 1878–81 WNC distiller society by that
  name was found; "Whistling Gap" appears as a *place* in family accounts, not an organization.
- Parrish's given name **William** collides with nothing local; keep. **Joshua** collides with Joshua
  Perkins, an antebellum Cranberry iron speculator — harmless, and worth a shrug in the audit file.
