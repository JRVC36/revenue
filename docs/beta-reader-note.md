# Beta-Reader Note — *REVENUE*

**Status: v1 — this is the one-page note the Style Guide §6.1 (docs/beta-reader-note.md) requires, sent with any partial
manuscript. Fill the bracketed counts before sending; the questions themselves are the ones to ask.**

---

**To whoever is reading this: five minutes at the end, please.**

This is a draft of *REVENUE*, a historical novel set in a hollow of western North Carolina, 1878–1881,
about the whiskey tax and the law that takes the land. **[X chapters, Y words]** of **[Z,000]** exist.
You are not being asked to copy-edit, and you are not being asked whether you liked it.

**What I am actually testing, in order:**

1. **The arithmetic.** Around the still and the ledger: did any passage make you stop and do math to
   check me, or feel that I was showing off? Point me at the sentence. If the corn/gallon/tax numbers
   landed without you noticing them, say so — that is the goal.
2. **The turn at the barn fire.** When the fire happens, did you understand *what had just become
   possible* for the family — or did it read as an escalation of violence? If the second, the legal
   mechanism hasn't been planted early enough and I need to know which earlier chapter felt thin.
3. **Whose book it feels like.** If you finished a chapter and thought "this is Joshua's book," or
   "I keep waiting to be inside Margaret's head," tell me which chapter. That is the only way I can
   find out whether the POV spine is holding.
4. **Where you put it down.** Not "got slow" — the actual last page you read before closing the file.
   Give me page numbers and I will thank you and disagree.
5. **The speech.** Do any of the mountain characters sound written, or sound like the author
   enjoying himself? Quote it. I have banned spelling-out dialect and I want to know if the ban is
   holding or if I moved the problem somewhere worse.
6. **One line you'd remember.** If there isn't one, say "there isn't one." That's the most useful
   answer on the page.

**Things you don't need to tell me:** whether the history is accurate (I have a period audit and a
linter; I'd rather you spend the time on the story), whether a scene is "too bleak," and what you think
the "message" is. If you think I've made a character preach, though, I want to know — that counts as #5.

There is one place where a reader may feel the book is explaining a law at them. If you felt it, name
the chapter; I would rather cut three pages than lose you there.

Thank you. Send it in any form — margin notes, a voice memo, a text at 11 p.m.

7. **The end, and how to react to it.** The book stops in **November 1881** and then goes out in **three
   documents with no author's note**: the agent's report to his Department (which spells the killed boy's name
   right), a county history (which gets the war's date wrong by two years), and the title page of a dime novel
   about Caleb. Nobody comes back to explain, and the railroad is never seen — a blast off a ridge near the
   opening and a blast off a ridge near the close. If the ending feels *withheld from*, that is a fair answer to
   a deliberate decision: tell me which page it started to feel that way on.

8. **One neighbor says *don't* at the meeting, and later loses his barn.** He is not a coward and not a
   traitor; he is the control group, and he is right. If you read him as a coward, that is a failure of the
   scene rather than of the man, and I want to know about it.
