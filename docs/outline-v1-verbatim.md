OUTLINE: "REVENUE"
A Three-Act Drama, 1878–1881




ACT ONE: THE THRESHOLD (1878–1879)


The World Before


Setting: A remote cove in northwestern North Carolina, near the Tennessee border, summer 1878. The ridges and hollows of the high country—Banner Elk, Elk Park, possibly fictionalized as "Elk Cove" or "North Fork Hollow."


Tone: Insular, self-governed, untroubled by distant law.


Opening image: JACOB BRENNAN (45–50), weathered and quiet, works a still in a hidden hollow. The still is not hidden in a paranoid way—it's just placed, the way you'd place a garden, where the water is right and the drainage works. His son MATTHEW (19) and his nephew CALEB (21) work with him. They're making whiskey the way Jacob's father made it, and his father before him. It's not criminal—it's just what you do.


They fill a jug and carry it down to a small settlement where a merchant—OLD HOSS TYLER—trades supplies. Jacob trades a jug for flour, salt, tobacco, cloth. It's a closed circuit. No money needs to change hands. No one outside the mountains needs to know about it.


The tone is peaceful, self-sufficient. This is a world that works.




The Arrival of the Excise


In autumn 1878, a man comes up from Morganton or from Johnson City. His name is WILLIAM PARRISH (35–40), a federal revenue agent deputized to enforce the whiskey tax.


He's not the first tax collector these people have seen—the property tax, the occasional excise—but he's the first who's serious. He has a rifle. He has a badge. He has orders from Washington to enforce the federal excise on spirits.


He doesn't come looking for Jacob immediately. First, he comes to town. He sets up an office. He talks to the judge, the sheriff, the leading men. He asks questions: Who makes whiskey? Where? How much?


He's met with silence. The leading men are civil but unhelpful. They don't make whiskey themselves, they say. They don't know who does. It's not their concern.


Parrish understands the situation: He cannot enforce the law without informants or without finding the stills himself. He needs a man on the inside—someone from the community who'll work for him.




The Recruitment of Joshua


Joshua Brennan (45), Jacob's older brother, is not a distiller. He's a trader and a mill operator—he grinds grain, he buys and sells goods, he's more integrated into the larger economy than Jacob is.


Parrish approaches him.


The conversation is businesslike. Parrish tells Joshua that the federal government is serious about collecting the tax. They're going to do it one way or another. If Joshua cooperates—if he tells Parrish where the stills are, who's running them, when the whiskey is being moved—Parrish will pay him. Good money. Better than he makes milling grain.


Joshua refuses.


Parrish says: "Then you're part of the conspiracy to defraud the United States government. That's a federal crime. I can charge you."


Joshua still refuses.


But something has shifted. The outside world has entered the cove. There's now a man with a gun and a legal authority who will not leave.




Jacob Understands


Jacob hears about Parrish from Joshua. The two brothers argue. Joshua says the government is serious, that resistance is pointless, that people will be arrested or killed if they fight it. Jacob says that this is their country, that the law is unjust, that they have a right to make whiskey.


Joshua says: "Right doesn't matter. They have guns and paper."


The argument is unresolved. But Jacob knows that the world has changed. He can:


Stop making whiskey and give up what's always been his livelihood
Keep making it and hope to avoid Parrish
Fight, which means organizing with other distillers, hiding the stills, lying to the revenue men, and potentially violence


Jacob chooses a version of 3.


He calls a meeting of the men who make whiskey—there are maybe a dozen families in the immediate area. They meet at night in a barn. The consensus is: they'll keep making whiskey. If Parrish comes, they'll handle it. They have numbers. They have knowledge of the country. They have a moral claim.


Caleb is the hothead. He says: "We should shoot him if he comes up here."


Jacob says: "No. We're not murderers. But we're not paying taxes on whiskey, either."




The Standoff Begins


Over the winter of 1878–79, Parrish makes occasional forays into the coves. He's looking for stills. He finds a few—abandoned or operated by desperate people who have no community protection. He pours out whiskey. He arrests one or two men.


But the main producers—Jacob and the other established operators—stay ahead of him. They move stills frequently. They have warning systems—a boy stationed on a ridge who'll signal if riders come up the hollow.


The community is bifurcated. Those who support Jacob and the distillers treat Parrish as an invader. Those who don't actively support him at least don't inform on him. Joshua, Jacob's brother, is in an agonizing middle position—he doesn't think Jacob is right, but he won't betray him.


Parrish is frustrated. He knows where the whiskey is coming from. He knows Jacob Brennan is the key operator. But without an informant, he can't prove it.




The Temptation and the Fall


In spring 1879, Parrish focuses on Matthew Brennan, Jacob's son.


Matthew is 19, ambitious, and not entirely convinced of his father's righteousness. Parrish approaches him with an offer: work for the government, tell me where your father's stills are, and I'll give you money and a chance to leave the mountains—go to Johnson City, get a real job, start a new life.


Matthew is tempted. The mountains are beautiful but suffocating. His father is a good man but stubborn. The whole community is united in a way that feels like a trap.


Matthew agrees.


Over the next weeks, Matthew feeds Parrish information. Parrish raids three stills. He finds Jacob's main operation—hidden in a laurel thicket up a hollow that only locals know about.


The stills are destroyed. The copper kettles are hauled away. Whiskey is poured out.


Jacob is arrested.




End of Act One


Jacob is taken to the federal courthouse in Asheville or Greeneville, Tennessee, to stand trial. He's charged with manufacturing and selling spirits without a federal tax stamp, conspiracy, and conspiracy to hinder a federal officer.


The charges carry prison time.


Caleb and the other distillers are furious. Someone informed. There's a meeting. Caleb is for finding out who and exacting justice. Jacob, even from custody, sends word: "Don't. Find out who it was, yes, but don't kill him. We're not murderers."


Joshua is heartsick. He knows it was Matthew, but he doesn't tell anyone. He goes to see Matthew, who's hiding at Parrish's house or in Johnson City. Joshua demands to know if it was him. Matthew doesn't deny it. Joshua tells him: "Your father will die in prison or on a road if you stay around here. You need to leave and never come back."


Matthew leaves North Carolina.


Margaret Brennan (Jacob's wife, 40s) sits in the courthouse in Asheville, watching her husband be sentenced. No one from the cove comes to support him publicly—that would be dangerous. But there's a moment when he's led out, and the courtroom empties, and everyone nods at Margaret. They haven't abandoned him.


Final image of Act One: Jacob in a jail cell, stone walls, a small window. He can see a ridge in the distance that looks like home. Parrish stands outside the cell. "You could have done this easier," Parrish says. Jacob doesn't answer.




ACT TWO: THE RECKONING (1879–1880)


The Empty Year


Jacob is sentenced to two years in federal prison. He's sent to a penitentiary in Kentucky or Virginia—somewhere far enough away that he can't be rescued and close enough to be a legal example.


In the cove, a strange thing happens: the whiskey business continues.


Caleb takes over. He's younger, angrier, and he's learned from Jacob's capture. The stills move more often. There are better warning systems. The entire community is organized in a way it wasn't before—not just the distillers, but their families, their neighbors, everyone. They treat Parrish as a common enemy.


But the informant is still in the community, in a sense. Parrish has other contacts. There are desperate people, people with debts, people with family feuds who might sell information for a few dollars.


Joshua is tormented. He knows Matthew betrayed his father, but he's also Joshua's nephew. He knows where Matthew is—probably working on a railroad in Virginia or Kentucky, trying to disappear. He wonders if he should tell Caleb and the others the truth, or let them search blindly for an unknown informant.


He decides to tell Caleb. Not in anger, but to stop the bloodshed that will come if they blame the wrong person.




The Name


Caleb and three other men ride down to Johnson City, find Matthew working on the railroad, and confront him.


Matthew admits it. He's not proud, but he's not fully repentant either. He says: "I did what I thought was right. Your father was breaking the law."


Caleb says: "My father was feeding his family. You sold him to the government for money."


They beat Matthew. It's not gentle—they leave him with broken ribs and a broken arm—but they don't kill him. Caleb says: "You're a Brennan. That's the only reason you're alive. If you come back to the cove, we will kill you."


Matthew never returns.




The Burning


Parrish, understanding the level of community organization now, decides to raid not just individual stills but to make an example.


In late 1879 or early 1880, he leads a group of deputies and local volunteers (men he's hired or coerced into helping) to raid a location where he knows whiskey is being stored before shipment.


It's a barn belonging to a man named WILLIAM HARPER (60), who's not a distiller himself but is part of the network—his barn is used for storage.


The deputies find the whiskey. They destroy it. They set fire to the barn—not on Parrish's orders explicitly, but it happens.


The barn burns. It's night. People pour out of their homes. They watch the barn—a landmark, a structure that's been part of the landscape—burn.


William Harper stands watching with his sons. He's old. The barn was his. It represents a life's work.


Caleb is there, among the crowd. He's watching a line being crossed. This isn't law enforcement anymore. This is destruction, punishment, collective injury.


Something crystallizes in Caleb: this will end in blood.




The Network Spreads


News of the burned barn spreads through the mountains. It reaches other coves, other counties, where men are also making whiskey and being pressured by revenue agents.


A loose network forms. Distillers in different areas communicate. They share information about Parrish's movements. They coordinate. Some of them are part of a more formal organization—there are rumors of a group called "the Whistlers" or some other name, though historians aren't entirely sure how organized they were.


Joshua is approached by Caleb with a proposal: we need you. You have connections in Johnson City and Morganton. You trade with merchants. You hear things. We need to know where Parrish is, what he's planning, who else might inform.


Joshua refuses. He says: "I won't be part of killing a man."


Caleb says: "We're not planning to kill anyone. But we need to know what's coming."


Joshua: "That's a lie. I know it, you know it. And I won't do it."


But Joshua is now isolated from both sides. The distillers think he's sided with the government. Parrish knows Joshua won't inform on his own family, so he's useless.




The First Blood


In early 1880, a revenue deputy is ambushed on a mountain road. The ambush is rough, not well-planned—three men on horseback, they fire at the deputy's horse, they chase him. He gets away, but barely.


No one is arrested. No one talks about who did it.


But the line has been crossed. It's no longer just property destruction and passive resistance. It's violence.


Parrish is alarmed and energized. He reports to his superiors in Washington that there's an organized conspiracy against federal authority and that he needs more resources. He gets them—more deputies, authority to deputize locals, orders to be aggressive.


The mountain is now under siege.




Jacob's Letters


From prison, Jacob writes letters to Margaret. They're smuggled out by a sympathetic guard and passed hand to hand through the mountains.


In one letter, he tells her: "Tell Caleb and the boys to stop. Tell them nothing is worth what's coming. The government has more guns and more patience. We'll wait. When I get out, we'll figure out a way to live that doesn't involve the federal men. But I won't come home to find my son dead."


Margaret reads this to Caleb. Caleb is silent. He doesn't argue, but he doesn't agree either.


He's trapped. His father is in prison because of what the government did. The government is tightening its grip. And the only way to push back is through violence, which his father forbids.




End of Act Two


By late 1880, the situation is unresolved and unbearable.


Jacob is still in prison, with about a year left on his sentence.
Parrish is entrenched, with more resources and more authority.
Caleb and the distillers have committed themselves to resistance, including violence.
Joshua is broken, living a half-life between worlds.
Matthew is gone, working himself to death on a railroad, trying to disappear.


Final image of Act Two: Caleb sitting on a ridge at night, looking down at the lights of Parrish's office in the small town below. He's holding a rifle. A man is beside him—one of the other distillers—asking: "What do we do?"


Caleb says: "We end it."




ACT THREE: THE RECKONING (1880–1881)


The Plan


Caleb and a small group—maybe four or five men, including the hotheads and the desperate—decide to confront Parrish directly.


There are different ways this could play out. Here's one:


They plan to ambush him on a road where he's known to travel regularly between towns. They'll force him off the road, confront him, and either force him to leave the mountains or... it's left unsaid, but everyone knows the possibility.


Joshua finds out about the plan through his network of connections. He's distraught. He goes to Caleb and says: "You can't do this. If you kill a federal agent, the Army will come. They'll burn every cabin in this cove."


Caleb says: "He's burning our barns."


Joshua says: "That's not the same. Once you cross into murder, there's no coming back."


But Caleb can't see another way.




The Ambush (Failed or Partial)


The ambush happens. Parrish is riding toward a town. Three or four armed men step out of the tree line.


The encounter is chaotic. Shots are fired. Parrish's horse is hit. Parrish is hit—wounded in the arm or leg, enough to be serious but not fatal. His deputies (he has one or two with him) return fire.


In the confusion, someone dies. It could be one of the distillers, or it could be one of the deputies. The exact identity matters for the drama, but not for the structure.


Parrish escapes. He's bleeding, but he gets to a town and medical help.


The distillers scatter. Caleb is among them, unharmed but shaken.




The Manhunt


The failed assassination attempt changes everything.


Federal marshals arrive from Johnson City or a larger city. They're now looking not just to enforce the tax but to arrest people for attempted murder of a federal officer—a serious crime with serious penalties.


Caleb becomes a hunted man. He can't stay in the cove because deputies will come. He can't leave because his family is there.


There's a choice: turn himself in, or run and hide indefinitely in the mountains.


He chooses to run. He becomes, for a time, an outlaw.


Joshua helps him. Despite everything, Joshua can't let his nephew die. He provides supplies, shelter, information. This makes Joshua a fugitive accessory.




The Capture (or Surrender)


The play can go a few directions here. One:


After weeks or months of hiding, pursued, sleeping in caves and hollow trees, eating what's brought to him, Caleb is exhausted. He's wanted for attempted murder. There's a price on his head. Other distillers have been arrested. The network is collapsing.


He turns himself in. Not to Parrish, but to the federal court, where at least he'll get a trial.


He's convicted and sentenced to five years in a federal penitentiary.


An alternative:


Caleb is captured in a dawn raid on his hiding place. A deputy shoots him—not fatally, but a serious wound. He's arrested and charged.


Either way, Caleb is imprisoned.




Jacob's Release and Return


Jacob is released from prison in late 1880 or early 1881. He returns to the mountains to find that:


Caleb is imprisoned for attempted murder of a federal officer
The local distilling network has been disrupted by raids and arrests
Parrish is still in place, vindicated, enforcing the tax


Jacob is a broken man. His sacrifice—his willingness to be imprisoned, to let Caleb take over—has come to nothing.


He goes to see Caleb in jail (or on the way to jail). Caleb says: "I'm sorry, Father. I tried to stop it, but I couldn't."


Jacob says: "I know. Sometimes you can't stop what's coming. You just have to survive it."




The Broader Tragedy


The play pulls back to show the larger movement:


Across North Carolina, Tennessee, and other Appalachian states, revenue enforcement is a constant low-level war. There are multiple incidents of violence—some distillers are killed, some deputies are killed, some operations are captured.


The federal government is winning. Not decisively, not quickly, but they're winning. The combination of tax enforcement, jail time, and intimidation is breaking the resistance.


Smaller distillers quit and find other work. Some move west to Kentucky or Missouri. Some take jobs in the mines or the mills. The old economy is ending.




Joshua's End


Joshua Brennan is left in an impossible position. He's not imprisoned—he never committed a direct crime—but he's known to have helped Caleb. The distillers resent him for refusing to fight. Parrish resents him for helping the fugitive.


He decides to leave. He sells his mill and his property and moves to Johnson City or another town, where he can start over as a merchant or a mill operator, away from the mountains, away from the history.


Before he leaves, he visits Margaret (Jacob's wife, now old and worn). He tells her he's sorry about Matthew, about Caleb, about everything.


Margaret says: "It was the government's war, not ours. We were just trying to live."




The Final Scene(s)


Option 1: Jacob at Home


Jacob sits on his porch, alone. The still is gone—he'll never make whiskey again. His son is in prison. The world has moved on.


A neighbor passes and nods. That's all the communication they have now—a nod.


Option 2: Caleb in Prison


A cell. Caleb is working (prison labor). He looks older than he is. In a voiceover, he thinks about home, about the mountains, about the fight that couldn't be won.


Option 3: Parrish's Victory


Parrish is in an office, looking at records. The number of seized stills has increased. The number of convictions has increased. He's been promoted or given a larger territory.


But there's a weariness about him too. It's a hollow victory—he's won by attrition and law, not by being welcomed.
